# FX evidence sweep — the raw findings
Seven independent research agents, one per angle, each instructed to report contradicting evidence with the same energy as supporting evidence, to label source quality honestly, and to answer whether each effect survives **retail** costs.
**Method limitation, stated up front:** this session's egress proxy blocked `WebFetch` for every domain attempted. Agents could run web searches and read titles, abstracts and snippets, and in some cases full Federal Reserve working-paper versions, but mostly could not open the published articles. Every claim below carries the agent's own verification label. Treat abstract-level claims as second-hand until checked against the article.
**Scale:** 7 angles, 95 claims.

## CHART PATTERNS in FX — head-and-shoulders, double tops, flags, triangles, and candlestick patterns (engulfing, piercing line, dark cloud cover, hammer, doji): what peer-reviewed work actually tested, on which market, with what win rate / payoff / expectancy, and whether it survives retail costs and data-snooping correction.

METHOD CAVEAT THAT AFFECTS EVERY QUALITY LABEL BELOW: in this session WebFetch was blocked by the network egress proxy for every domain attempted (nber.org, ssrn.com, onlinelibrary.wiley.com, sciencedirect, journals.sagepub.com, academic.oup.com, newyorkfed.org, files.stlouisfed.org, ideas.repec.org, arxiv.org, semanticscholar.org, cis.upenn.edu, biz.uiowa.edu, en.wikipedia.org). I could run WebSearch but could not open a single full paper. So every "peer-reviewed" label below means "the paper is real, published in the named venue, and I verified its headline finding from abstracts and search summaries" — NOT "I read the tables." Specific numbers I could not verify are marked as missing rather than guessed.

TWO SEARCH-ENGINE CONFLATIONS I CAUGHT, so they don't propagate: (1) A search summary attributed to Chang & Osler "strong evidence of economically significant out-of-sample excess returns for each of six exchange rates ($/DM, $/Yen, $/SF, $/£, DM/Yen, SF/£), 1981–95." That is the sample design of Neely, Weller & Dittmar's genetic-programming paper, not Chang & Osler's H&S paper. Do not cite it as H&S evidence. (2) A search summary claimed Lo/Mamaysky/Wang found "statistically significant abnormal returns of 1%–3%" including for "flags." LMW did not test flags and their published claim is about conditional return DISTRIBUTIONS, not abnormal returns. Both look like model-generated blending. Treated as unverified and excluded.

BOTTOM LINE FOR THE TRADER: the honest answer to "which chart pattern has the highest win rate in FX" is that the question has no evidence-based answer. Only head-and-shoulders has a real peer-reviewed FX literature and it is split, with the more rigorous and more recent side negative. Candlesticks have been tested properly in equities and futures and failed there; the only positive FX candlestick paper I found is low-tier and uncorrected. Flags, pennants, triangles, wedges and double tops have, as far as I could find, never been tested in peer-reviewed work ON FX at all. And not one FX chart-pattern study I found reports win rate and payoff ratio together, which is the only pair of numbers that would answer the question as asked.

### Head-and-shoulders is the ONLY classical chart pattern with a substantial peer-reviewed literature testing it on FX. Flags, pennants, triangles, wedges, rectangles and double tops/bottoms have no peer-reviewed FX profitability tests that I could locate.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (for what exists, abstract-level verification only) + absence-of-evidence finding from systematic search; the '88% double bottom' counter-example is vendor/blog

**Evidence:** Repeated targeted searches for FX tests of flags/pennants/triangles/double tops returned only (a) vendor and broker education pages, (b) equity-market studies, or (c) the Tsinaslanidis/Zapranis pattern-recognition line of work, which is applied to NASDAQ/NYSE stocks and US tech stocks, not currencies. Tsinaslanidis & Zapranis's own results on the equity side are weak: their support/resistance method 'failed to generate excess returns in comparison with simple buy-and-hold strategies,' and their saucer/resistance strategy on seven US tech stocks produced positive excess returns 'only in the first subperiods of examination (the 1990s).' The Lo/Mamaysky/Wang pattern set (head-and-shoulders, inverse H&S, broadening tops/bottoms, triangle tops/bottoms, rectangle tops/bottoms, double tops/bottoms) is US equities 1962–1996 and has not to my knowledge been replicated on FX. A search for a 'double top / double bottom' FX peer-reviewed test returned zero academic hits and surfaced instead an unsourced vendor claim that the double bottom is 'an 88% success pattern in recent backtests' — with no paper, no sample, no cost model, no payoff figure behind it.

**Against:** I cannot prove a negative from search alone. Non-English, paywalled, or conference literature testing triangles/flags on FX may exist and would not surface in these searches. Also, Hsu, Taylor & Wang (2016, J. International Economics) DID find substantial predictability across 30 developed and emerging currencies over 45 years using a stepwise data-snooping test on 21,000+ rules — but those are parametric RULES (moving average, filter, channel, oscillator), not visually-defined chart patterns, so it is not evidence for flags or triangles.

**Survives retail costs?** Not applicable — there is no measured effect to cost-adjust. This is the single most important finding for the trader: a system built on flags/triangles/double tops in FX is not 'supported by weak evidence,' it is built on no published FX evidence at all. Every 'win rate' number circulating for these patterns in FX comes from vendor backtests or from Bulkowski's stock data.

**Sources:** <https://dspace.lib.uom.gr/bitstream/2159/15200/2/TsinaslanidisProdromos_Phd2012.pdf>, <https://www.nber.org/papers/w7613>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022199616300472>

### Chang & Osler (1999, Economic Journal) — the paper usually cited as PRO head-and-shoulders in FX — actually found the pattern profitable for only two of five dollar exchange rates, and concluded the rule is DOMINATED by simple filter rules, i.e. it fails their efficiency test.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Economic Journal; also NY Fed Staff Report No. 4). Abstract-level verification only — full text blocked.

**Evidence:** Chang & Osler, 'Methodical Madness: Technical Analysis and the Irrationality of Exchange-rate Forecasts,' Economic Journal 109, 636–652 (1999). Daily dollar rates, March 1973 – June 1994, H&S identified by an objective computer algorithm built from published charting manuals, profits compared against 10,000 bootstrap series under a random-walk null. Findings as verified from abstracts: H&S trading 'generates statistically significant profits,' satisfying their first rationality condition (profitability); but 'the trading rule is dominated by simple filter rules, so it does not satisfy the second condition' (efficiency). Forecasting power was present for the German mark and yen but NOT for the Canadian dollar, Swiss franc, or French franc.

**Against:** This is itself a heavily qualified result, and it is contradicted from three directions. (1) Lucke (2003) tested many H&S definitional variants on FX and found returns not significantly positive, with any non-zero evidence pointing NEGATIVE. (2) Osler's own later work (1998) found H&S unprofitable in US equities. (3) The 3-of-5-currencies failure is itself a multiple-comparisons red flag: testing five currencies and reporting the two that worked is exactly the pattern data-snooping corrections exist to penalise, and the paper predates the White Reality Check (2000) and Hansen SPA (2005), so no such correction was applied.

**Survives retail costs?** Almost certainly not, on the paper's own logic. If a complex pattern rule is dominated by a simple filter rule on GROSS terms, it is dominated by more on NET terms, because H&S entails more discretionary entries and wider stops around a neckline. I could NOT verify the paper's transaction-cost assumption, its win rate, its average win, its average loss, or its Sharpe ratio — none of those surfaced, and I will not invent them. Note also the sample ends 1994; Neely, Weller & Ulrich (2009) show FX technical profits from that era had decayed to zero by the early 1990s.

**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1468-0297.00466>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=51421>, <https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fednsr/4.html>

### Lucke (2003, Applied Economics) is a direct, dedicated FX test of head-and-shoulders trading rules and it is a clean NEGATIVE: returns are not significantly positive, and to the extent there is any evidence of non-zero returns, it is evidence of NEGATIVE returns.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Applied Economics). Abstract-level verification only.

**Evidence:** Bernd Lucke, 'Are technical trading rules profitable? Evidence for head-and-shoulder rules,' Applied Economics 35(1), 33–40 (2003). The design is the important part: rather than testing one hand-picked H&S definition, Lucke decomposed the pattern into its building blocks (smoothed trends, trend reversal, resistance levels, volatility clustering) and tested many combinations. Verified finding: 'For various combinations of the building blocks of head-and-shoulder definitions the result is generally negative: Returns to head-and-shoulder trading rules are not significantly positive - and if there is any evidence for non-zero returns at all, then it is evidence for negative returns.'

**Against:** Chang & Osler (1999) found significant gross profits for DEM and JPY over 1973–94. The two can be partially reconciled: different sample, different H&S definition, and Lucke's point is precisely that the result is fragile to how you define the pattern. That fragility is the finding. A pattern whose measured profitability flips sign depending on which neckline/shoulder tolerance you pick is not a tradable edge — it is a specification choice.

**Survives retail costs?** Irrelevant — the gross result is already zero-to-negative. Costs make it worse. This is the most decision-relevant single paper for a retail trader planning to build an FX system on head-and-shoulders.

**Sources:** <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00036840210150884>, <https://econpapers.repec.org/article/tafapplec/v_3a35_3ay_3a2003_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a33-40.htm>

### Osler (1998) found that head-and-shoulders traders in US equities are NOISE TRADERS: the pattern generates a large spike in trading volume but does not profitably predict direction, and its price impact decays within about two weeks.

**Quality:** working-paper (Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report — central-bank research, not a refereed journal). Abstract-level verification only.

**Evidence:** Carol Osler, 'Identifying Noise Traders: The Head-and-Shoulders Pattern in U.S. Equities,' Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report No. 42 (1998). Verified findings: agents speculating on H&S are identified as noise traders because (1) trading volume is exceptionally high when they are active and (2) their trading is unprofitable. H&S sales lower prices and H&S purchases raise them, 'effects that disappear within two weeks.' The pattern 'is associated with a substantial rise in trading volume even though it does not profitably predict directional movements,' and is associated with LOWER bid-ask spreads.

**Against:** This is a US EQUITY result, not FX, so it does not directly refute Chang & Osler's FX finding — and it is by the same author, who found partial FX profitability. Savin, Weller & Zvingelis (2007) later found H&S in US stocks DOES have predictive power for excess returns (5–7%/yr risk-adjusted when conditioned on the pattern), which cuts against Osler's flat 'unprofitable' on equities. But note Savin et al. simultaneously found 'little or no support for the profitability of a stand-alone trading strategy' — so the two agree on the thing that matters to a trader.

**Survives retail costs?** No. Osler's result is that the pattern's price effect fully mean-reverts inside two weeks, meaning any apparent move is temporary impact from the pattern-traders themselves, not information. Retail traders following the pattern are the supply of liquidity, not the consumers of it. The lower-bid-ask finding is a further tell: these are easy, uninformed flows for a dealer to fill.

**Sources:** <https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr42.html>, <https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr42.pdf>, <https://academic.oup.com/jfec/article-abstract/5/2/243/785044>

### Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000) — the paper most often waved at as academic proof that chart patterns work — is a US EQUITY paper, makes no profitability claim, and its own authors warn that pattern detection and trading profit are different objectives.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Finance — top-tier). Abstract- and secondary-level verification only; full text blocked.

**Evidence:** Lo, Mamaysky & Wang, 'Foundations of Technical Analysis,' Journal of Finance 55(4), 1705–1765 (2000); NBER WP 7613. Method: nonparametric kernel-regression smoothing to detect patterns automatically, US stocks 1962–1996. Patterns tested: head-and-shoulders and inverse H&S, broadening tops/bottoms, triangle tops/bottoms, rectangle tops/bottoms, double tops/bottoms. The verified claim is distributional, not economic: comparing the unconditional empirical distribution of daily returns to the distribution conditioned on a pattern, 'several technical indicators do provide incremental information and may have some practical value.' The authors explicitly note that 'patterns that are optimal for detecting statistical anomalies need not be optimal for indicating trading profits, and vice versa.' No win rate, no payoff ratio, no expectancy, no transaction costs, no FX.

**Against:** Jegadeesh's published discussion in the same issue of the Journal of Finance is critical, faulting the paper for providing no theoretical reason why historical price patterns should be expected to repeat when market conditions change. Separately, the search summary claiming LMW found '1%–3% abnormal returns' for 'head-and-shoulders, double tops, double bottoms, and flags' is NOT supportable — LMW did not test flags and reported distribution tests, not abnormal returns. Treat that number, wherever the trader encounters it, as fabricated. And critically: rejecting equality of two return distributions with 31 years and thousands of stocks is a statement about statistical power, not about money.

**Survives retail costs?** The paper does not address costs and does not claim to. Converting a distributional difference into a net-of-cost FX strategy is an unbridged gap that nobody, as far as I can find, has crossed for currencies.

**Sources:** <https://www.nber.org/papers/w7613>, <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-1082.00265>, <https://stuff.mit.edu/people/wangj/pap/LoMamayskyWang00.pdf>

### Savin, Weller & Zvingelis (2007) — the most favourable rigorous head-and-shoulders paper — still found NO support for a stand-alone H&S trading strategy. Its headline 5–7%/yr is a conditional, risk-adjusted excess return, not a tradable stand-alone result, and it is on US stocks, not FX.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Financial Econometrics). Abstract-level verification only.

**Evidence:** Savin, Weller & Zvingelis, 'The Predictive Power of Head-and-Shoulders Price Patterns in the U.S. Stock Market,' Journal of Financial Econometrics 5(2), 243–265 (2007). Used the Lo/Mamaysky/Wang recognition algorithm modified with filters derived from a practising technical analyst's criteria. Data: S&P 500 and Russell 2000, 1990–1999. Verified findings: 'little or no support for the profitability of a stand-alone trading strategy'; but 'strong evidence that the pattern had power to predict excess returns,' with risk-adjusted excess returns to a strategy conditioned on H&S of 5–7% per year.

**Against:** Osler (1998) found H&S in US equities unprofitable outright. Ahlawat (per secondary citation I could not verify at source) reports that across a cross-section of stocks and indices 'no pattern produces statistically and economically significant profits,' though a few are better predictors than others. And the sample is a single decade (1990–99) in one market — the same decade in which Neely/Weller/Ulrich show FX rule profits had already died, and in which Tsinaslanidis & Zapranis found their equity pattern edges existed only in the early subperiod.

**Survives retail costs?** The authors' own answer is effectively no for a stand-alone strategy. I could not verify what transaction cost they assumed, and no win-rate/payoff decomposition surfaced. For an FX trader this paper is doubly inapplicable: wrong asset class, and the surviving effect is conditional rather than executable.

**Sources:** <https://academic.oup.com/jfec/article-abstract/5/2/243/785044>, <https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jfinec/v5yi2p243-265.html>

### Candlestick patterns fail in every market where they have been tested against a properly randomised null. Marshall, Young & Rose (2006) generated random open/high/low/close series by bootstrap and found candlestick strategies have NO value on DJIA stocks; Fock, Klein & Zwergel (2005) tested 19 patterns on intraday DAX index futures and Bund futures with negative results; Marshall, Young & Cahan (2008) found no value in Japanese equities 1975–2004.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Banking & Finance; Journal of Derivatives; SAGE Open). Abstract- and secondary-level verification only.

**Evidence:** Marshall, Young & Rose, 'Candlestick technical trading strategies: Can they create value for investors?', Journal of Banking & Finance 30, 2303–2323 (2006) — the key methodological contribution is EXTENDING the bootstrap to generate random OHLC (not just close) series, because candlesticks use all four prices, so a close-only bootstrap cannot form a valid null; result: 14 candlestick patterns tested, no predictive power or financial value on DJIA stocks; 'candlestick technical analysis does not create value for investors in informationally efficient markets.' Fock, Klein & Zwergel, 'Performance of Candlestick Analysis on Intraday Futures Data,' Journal of Derivatives 13(1), 28–40 (2005) — 19 patterns on German index and government bond futures intraday, negative. Marshall, Young & Cahan (2008) — Japanese equity market 1975–2004, no evidence of value. Tharavanij, Siraprapasiri & Rajchamaha (2017, SAGE Open) on the Stock Exchange of Thailand — most patterns not statistically different from zero, and candlestick analysis 'was not helpful for the majority of stocks for any of the sub-periods whether in a bull or bear market.'

**Against:** Some positive candlestick results exist in Asian equities — one Taiwan study reports four patterns profitable after transaction costs with bootstrap, out-of-sample and sub-sample robustness checks, and Deng et al. (2022) test SSE50 component stocks. So candlesticks are not universally dead. But note the pattern of the pattern: the positives cluster in less liquid, less arbitraged equity markets, and the methodologically strongest tests (OHLC bootstrap on DJIA; intraday futures) are negative. Crucially, NONE of the positive results are on major FX pairs, which are the most liquid and most arbitraged markets on earth.

**Survives retail costs?** Mostly moot — the gross results are already null in the strongest tests. Where positives exist they are in markets with different cost structures than retail FX. For the specific patterns the trader named: engulfing, piercing line, dark cloud cover, hammer and doji are all inside the pattern sets that failed in Marshall/Young/Rose and Fock/Klein/Zwergel.

**Sources:** <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378426605002116>, <https://www.pm-research.com/content/iijderiv/13/1/28>, <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5157791_Are_candlestick_technical_trading_strategies_profitable_in_the_Japanese_equity_market>, <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2158244017736799>

### The only FX-specific candlestick study I could find reporting positive results (Yassini, Roodposhti & Fallahshams, 2019) is published in a low-tier journal, uses plain z-tests with no data-snooping correction, applies no transaction-cost adjustment, and reports no payoff ratio — so it cannot support a 'high win rate' claim.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed but LOW-TIER (International Journal of Finance and Managerial Accounting). I could not open it; verification is abstract/secondary only. Treat as weak.

**Evidence:** 'Analyzing the Effectiveness of Candlestick Technical Trading Strategies in Foreign Exchange Market,' International Journal of Finance and Managerial Accounting (2019). Design as verified: four bullish and four bearish Japanese candlestick reversal patterns across seven currencies spanning advanced and emerging markets — AUD/USD, USD/CAD, EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/INR, USD/JPY, USD/ZAR; 12-year span, 3,129 observations; 'z score' test for statistical significance at the 5% level across seven holding periods. Reported result: some patterns (e.g. bullish hammer, bearish hanging man) show statistically significant returns at 5%. A related FX candlestick paper notes bullish reversal patterns profitable prior to trend, most bearish reversal patterns showing predictive power, with effective holding periods clustering around 10–15 days.

**Against:** Directly contradicted by the stronger candlestick literature above (Marshall/Young/Rose OHLC bootstrap; Fock/Klein/Zwergel intraday futures). Three specific methodological problems make the positive finding unreliable: (1) 8 patterns × 7 currencies × 7 holding periods ≈ 392 tests at the 5% level, so roughly 20 'significant' results are expected by chance alone with no real effect, and no White Reality Check / Hansen SPA / stepwise correction was applied; (2) no out-of-sample split reported; (3) a 10–15 day holding period on a spot FX position incurs 10–15 swap/rollover charges, which the study does not net out. Separately, a comparative FX study across the eight most-traded pairs over 13 years concluded that Doji patterns are 'unreliable indicators of reversals in the FOREX market' — a direct negative on one of the patterns the trader named.

**Survives retail costs?** Not demonstrated, and probably not. A 10–15 day hold means swap is a first-order cost, not a rounding error: on a negative-carry pair a retail trader can pay materially more in rollover over two weeks than the pattern's entire measured edge. The study reports returns but no win rate paired with a payoff ratio, so expectancy cannot be reconstructed from it.

**Sources:** <http://www.ijfma.ir/article_15259_f72b2d6cab8a8deb885ffecc523e4294.pdf>, <https://ph01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/ecticit/article/view/256994>

### WIN RATE IS NOT EDGE, and FX has the cleanest possible demonstration of this: the broker's own client data shows a 61% win rate on EUR/USD that still loses money. Average winner 48 pips, average loser 83 pips implies expectancy of about MINUS 3.1 pips per trade BEFORE spread.

**Quality:** industry (broker's own client data, not peer-reviewed, not independently audited). Weight it as suggestive, not definitive — but note it cuts AGAINST the broker's commercial interest in selling trading, which is the direction of bias that makes broker data more credible, not less. The cost figures are vendor/blog quality.

**Evidence:** FXCM 'Traits of Successful Traders' (broker's own aggregated client trade data): EUR/USD trades closed at a profit 61% of the time, but the average losing trade was 83 pips while the average winner was only 48 pips — 'traders lost 70% more on their losing trades than they won on winning trades.' My arithmetic on their figures: expectancy = (0.61 × 48) − (0.39 × 83) = 29.28 − 32.37 = −3.09 pips per trade gross. Add a realistic all-in EUR/USD retail cost of roughly 0.8–1.0 pip round-trip (raw spread ~0.1 pip plus ~$5/side commission per standard lot, i.e. ~$10 round-turn ≈ 1.0 pip) and expectancy is roughly −4 pips per trade net. The same dataset shows the fix is payoff, not accuracy: of traders who used a risk/reward of 1:1 or better, 53% turned a profit; of those who did not, only 17% did.

**Against:** No contradicting evidence found — and per the brief's own rule, that should raise suspicion rather than confidence. The honest caveat is that this is a single broker's client base in one era, self-published, with no disclosed methodology, sample size, date range, or survivorship treatment; I could not verify the '12 million trades' figure that circulates alongside it. It is an illustration of the arithmetic, not a parameter estimate.

**Survives retail costs?** This IS the cost-survival calculation, and the answer is no. The structural point generalises: on a 30-pip stop at 1:1, a 1-pip round-trip cost is ~3.3% of risk and pushes the break-even win rate from 50.0% to about 51.7%. On a 10-pip stop it is 10% of risk and pushes break-even from 50.0% to about 55%. So the tighter the stop — and high-win-rate pattern plans are almost always tight-stop plans — the more of the 'edge' the spread eats. A 90%-win plan with a 1:9 payoff has break-even at exactly 90% and is killed outright by any cost at all.

**Sources:** <https://docs.fxcorporate.com/fxcm-traits-of-successful-traders-guide.pdf>, <https://www.fxcm.com/markets/trading-guides/traits-successful-traders/>, <https://www.oanda.com/us-en/trading/historical-spreads/>

### Data-snooping correction is what kills FX technical results, and when it is applied properly the profits mostly vanish. Kuang, Schröder & Wang (2014) tested 25,988 rules on ten emerging FX markets, found annual mean excess returns above 30% for the best rules, and showed almost all of it disappears once data-snooping bias is accounted for.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (International Journal of Forecasting; Journal of Money, Credit and Banking; Journal of Economic Surveys). Abstract-level verification only.

**Evidence:** Kuang, Schröder & Wang, 'Illusory profitability of technical analysis in emerging foreign exchange markets,' International Journal of Forecasting (2014). Verified findings: 25,988 trading rules across ten emerging FX markets; best rules generate annual mean excess return of more than 30%; hundreds to thousands of seemingly significant profitable strategies under standard tests; 'almost all of these profits vanish once the data snooping bias is taken into account' — profitability is 'illusory'; findings hold across different transaction-cost assumptions, sub-samples, and reduced rule universes. Supporting: Qi & Wu (2006, Journal of Money, Credit and Banking) applied White's Reality Check to 2,127 FX rules — snooping bias did not overturn the full-sample conclusion but the problem was 'more serious in the second half of the sample,' with profitability 'much weaker in the more recent period.' Park & Irwin (2007, Journal of Economic Surveys) surveyed 95 modern studies: 56 positive, 20 negative, 19 mixed — but 'most empirical studies are subject to various problems in their testing procedures, e.g., data snooping, ex post selection of trading rules or search technologies, and difficulties in estimation of risk and transaction costs.'

**Against:** Hsu, Taylor & Wang (2016, Journal of International Economics) is the strongest counter-evidence: 45 years of daily data, 30 developed and emerging currencies, 21,000+ rules, a STEPWISE test explicitly designed to counter data-snooping bias, plus out-of-sample cross-validation — and they still find 'substantial predictability and excess profitability' in both developed and emerging currencies. So the snooping-correction literature is not unanimous. Two things to hold alongside it: their result concerns parametric rules, not chart patterns, and their own interpretation attributes the predictability to 'temporarily not-fully-rational behavior and market immaturity,' i.e. conditions that erode.

**Survives retail costs?** Kuang et al. explicitly report their conclusion holds 'when considering different transaction costs.' The relevant lesson for pattern-based system building: the 56-vs-20 positive/negative tally in Park & Irwin is not a vote count in favour of technical analysis — it is a publication-bias and specification-search artifact that the snooping literature was invented to measure.

**Sources:** <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207013000964>, <https://www.st-andrews.ac.uk/~wwwecon/repecfiles/2/1302.pdf>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=788013>, <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-6419.2007.00519.x>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022199616300472>

### FX technical trading profits were real in the 1970s–80s but had already disappeared by the early 1990s for the simple rules, in genuine out-of-sample tests. Any FX pattern study whose sample ends in the mid-1990s is describing a dead regime.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JFQA) plus central-bank working paper / handbook chapter. Abstract- and secondary-level verification only.

**Evidence:** Neely, Weller & Ulrich, 'The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis: Evidence from the Foreign Exchange Market,' Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis (2009). Verified findings: true out-of-sample tests on previously studied rules show the excess returns of the 1970s and 1980s 'were genuine and not just the result of data mining' — but 'these profit opportunities had disappeared by the early 1990s for filter and moving average rules.' Returns to less-studied rules also declined but 'have probably not completely disappeared.' Neely & Weller's survey (2011, FRB St. Louis WP 2011-001; Handbook of Exchange Rates) states that simple technical trading rules on dollar exchange rates 'provided 15 years of positive, risk-adjusted returns during the 1970s and 80s before those returns were extinguished,' and that conventional explanations based on risk adjustment or central bank intervention do not plausibly account for them.

**Against:** Hsu, Taylor & Wang (2016) find predictability persisting across 45 years including recent decades, which is the main dissent. Neely et al. themselves leave a door open for 'less-studied rules.' But the direction of the adaptive-markets prediction is explicit and unfavourable to a retail trader adopting a WELL-KNOWN pattern: 'the profitability of each strategy diminishes over time' and 'complex strategies can yield profits for longer than simpler ones.' Head-and-shoulders, engulfing candles and double tops are the most-published, most-taught, most-crowded patterns in existence — precisely the category predicted to be arbitraged away first.

**Survives retail costs?** The decay conclusion is about risk-adjusted excess returns and therefore already the relevant net concept. The timing point is decisive for reading the pattern literature: Chang & Osler's sample ends June 1994, Lo/Mamaysky/Wang's ends 1996, Savin et al. covers 1990–99. Every favourable chart-pattern result cited in this angle comes from inside or before the window that Neely/Weller/Ulrich show had already closed.

**Sources:** <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/adaptive-markets-hypothesis-evidence-from-the-foreign-exchange-market/9D336CDCA83233819EB5CDD0F4BC0DAA>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1403345>, <https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fedlwp/2011-001.html>

### The most robust technical finding in FX is NOT a chart pattern at all — it is Osler's (2003) microstructure result that stop-loss and take-profit orders cluster at round numbers, which mechanically produces support/resistance behaviour and price cascades.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Finance — top-tier), and unusually strong because it uses proprietary ORDER data rather than inferring from prices. Abstract-level verification only.

**Evidence:** Osler, 'Currency Orders and Exchange Rate Dynamics: An Explanation for the Predictive Success of Technical Analysis,' Journal of Finance 58(5), 1791–1819 (2003). Built on actual stop-loss and take-profit order data from a large FX dealing bank. Verified findings: requested execution rates are strongly clustered at round numbers, which are commonly used as support and resistance levels; take-profit orders tend to REVERSE price trends at those levels; stop-loss orders tend to INTENSIFY trends once levels are crossed. This explains two specific charting predictions — trends reverse at predictable support/resistance, and trends gain momentum once those levels break. Follow-up: Osler, 'Stop-loss orders and price cascades in currency markets,' Journal of International Money and Finance 24(2), 219–241 (2005).

**Against:** No direct contradiction found, which per the brief's rule warrants caution rather than confidence — though this paper's credibility rests on a data source (a dealing bank's order book) that is qualitatively different from the price-history studies, so it is less exposed to the snooping critique. Two real limitations: (1) it documents a MECHANISM, not a net-of-cost retail strategy, and it does not report win rate, payoff or expectancy for any trading rule; (2) the mechanism is double-edged for retail — the reason round numbers 'work' is that retail stops are sitting there, so a retail trader placing a stop just beyond a round number is supplying the very liquidity the cascade consumes.

**Survives retail costs?** Unknown and untested as a strategy. The honest reading is that this is the strongest evidence for WHERE in FX price structure something non-random happens (round numbers, order clustering) and simultaneously an argument against the named visual patterns, none of which this paper validates. If the trader wants the empirically best-supported technical concept in FX, it is round-number support/resistance and order-flow clustering — not head-and-shoulders, not engulfing candles.

**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1540-6261.00588>, <https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v58y2003i5p1791-1819.html>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261560604001147>

### The 'win rate' tables retail traders cite for chart patterns come overwhelmingly from Bulkowski's Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns, which is stock-only, defines success as a 5% move, measures exits at the 'ultimate high/low' (an unachievable perfect exit), and applies no transaction costs — so its percentages are not win rates in any tradable sense and do not transfer to FX.

**Quality:** industry / self-published (Bulkowski) for the win-rate tables; the criticisms are a mix of vendor/blog and peer-reviewed. Bulkowski's underlying data collection is large and serious, but the success metric is not an expectancy metric.

**Evidence:** Bulkowski's own site documents the methodology. Verified criticisms from search: success is defined as a 5% price change, described as 'a very low bar'; the ultimate high/low methodology 'doesn't translate into real trades, as they are perfect trades, instead of having a more real world use case'; the work 'is directed to stocks.' Academic counterweights on the same patterns: Savin et al. — 'We find little or no support for the profitability of a stand-alone [head and shoulders] trading strategy'; Ahlawat (via secondary citation) — 'no pattern produces statistically and economically significant profits for a cross-section of stocks and indices analyzed, though a few patterns are more successful predictors.'

**Against:** In fairness to Bulkowski: he does publish failure-rate studies and acknowledges methodological limits himself, and the dataset (tens of thousands of patterns) is far larger than most academic samples. The problem is not dishonesty, it is that a '5% move at some point before the ultimate reversal' statistic answers a different question than 'what is my expectancy per trade.' A pattern can post an 80%+ 'success rate' on that definition while having negative expectancy for anyone who must choose a stop and an exit in advance.

**Survives retail costs?** No — costs are not applied at all, and neither is slippage, nor a specified stop, nor a specified exit rule. And these are US EQUITY patterns: transferring a stock 'success rate' to FX ignores that FX has no volume data (which several candlestick and pattern definitions implicitly lean on), 24-hour continuous trading with no gaps to define reversal days, and overnight swap charges that equities do not have in the same form.

### The base rate for the population this trader is joining is catastrophic and is documented by regulators, not brokers: 74–89% of retail CFD/FX accounts lose money, with the French AMF finding 89% of clients lost an average of €10,900 over four years.

**Quality:** REGULATOR data — the highest-quality source class in this entire answer. Not peer-reviewed, but it is census-level administrative data from supervised firms, with no commercial interest in the result. Abstract/secondary verification only (AMF PDF not opened).

**Evidence:** AMF (Autorité des marchés financiers, France), study published 13 October 2014 covering 2009–2012: 14,799 active individual investors at authorised providers; 89% lost money; average loss approximately €10,900 per client; 13,224 customers lost €175 million in total while the remaining 1,575 earned only €13.8 million combined. The AMF also found no novice learning curve over time, and that 'the most active and regular traders saw their losses grow over time.' Mandatory broker disclosures since 2018 have kept loss rates in roughly the 65–80% range.

**Against:** No contradicting evidence found, and I looked. The one genuine nuance is a selection question: these are CFD/FX retail accounts broadly, not specifically traders running a disciplined chart-pattern system, so the base rate is not a direct prediction for any individual method. But the AMF finding that losses GREW with activity and experience is the specific finding that should worry anyone planning to trade patterns frequently — it is evidence against the 'I'll learn my way out of it' assumption that underwrites most retail system-building.

**Survives retail costs?** These figures are already net of all costs — that is exactly what makes them the right denominator. The AMF numbers embed spread, commission, swap and slippage as actually paid by real clients, which is why they are more decision-relevant than any gross backtest win rate.

### Professional FX participants overwhelmingly USE technical analysis, but this is evidence about behaviour and about why order-flow effects exist — it is not evidence that chart patterns are profitable, and it is routinely misused as if it were.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Economic Literature — top-tier survey journal). Abstract- and secondary-level verification only.

**Evidence:** Menkhoff & Taylor, 'The Obstinate Passion of Foreign Exchange Professionals: Technical Analysis,' Journal of Economic Literature 45(4), 936–972 (2007). Taylor & Allen (1992) found 90% of surveyed FX dealers report using some form of technical analysis. Menkhoff & Taylor evaluate four explanations for the persistence — that the market is not fully rational, that TA exploits official intervention, that it is an efficient form of information processing, and that it informs on non-fundamental influences — and judge the LAST the most plausible. Note what that means: the favoured explanation is that technical analysis is a proxy for order flow and non-fundamental positioning, not that geometric shapes forecast prices.

**Against:** The survey evidence is genuinely ambiguous and is cited by both camps. Menkhoff & Taylor themselves write that TA 'is widespread in the foreign exchange market and that it may be profitable,' which the pro-TA side quotes. The counter is that widespread usage is exactly what Osler (1998, 2003) predicts under a noise-trader / order-clustering model: everyone watching the same levels is the mechanism that makes those levels temporarily self-fulfilling AND the mechanism by which the crowd's stops get harvested. Popularity is consistent with both 'it works' and 'it is the liquidity.'

**Survives retail costs?** Not a cost question. Flagged because 'ninety percent of professional dealers use technical analysis' is the single most common argument a retail trader will meet when they ask whether patterns work, and it does not establish what it is used to establish. Dealers also earn the spread, see order flow, and hold positions for minutes — none of which a retail chart-pattern trader does.

## Peer-reviewed literature on whether technical trading rules are profitable in FX: the arc from early positive findings (1980s-90s filter and moving-average rules), through the data-snooping critiques (White's Reality Check, Hansen's SPA, stepwise tests), to the post-2000 collapse. I read six papers in full text (Neely/Weller/Ulrich 2007 WP, Neely & Weller 2011 survey, Neely & Weller 2003 intraday, Neely & Weller 2013 "Lessons", Hsu/Taylor/Wang 2016, Famiglietti/Ivanova/Neely/Weller 2020) totalling ~90k words, extracted via pdftotext, plus abstracts for papers whose hosts were blocked by the network egress proxy. Bottom line for a retail trader: the effect was real, it decayed to roughly zero in major currencies by the mid-1990s, the residual lives in emerging-market currencies that retail cannot cheaply trade, and the literature does not contain the concept of "win rate" at all.

### The peer-reviewed FX technical-trading literature does not report win rates, payoff ratios, or expectancy at all. There is no academic basis for ranking FX chart patterns or trading plans by win rate.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (six full texts, five of which are Fed working-paper versions of articles published in JFQA, JIMF, JIE, and the Wiley Handbook of Exchange Rates) — the absence finding is my own verified measurement over that corpus

**Evidence:** I extracted full text of six core papers (~95,600 words) and grep-searched for every win-rate-adjacent term. By contrast 'Sharpe' appears 226 times, 'excess return' 285 times, 'break-even [transaction cost]' 31 times, t-statistics 38 times. The field's entire metric vocabulary is mean excess return, Sharpe ratio, t-statistic, and break-even transaction cost. Papers searched: Neely/Weller/Ulrich (2007 WP 2006-046B), Neely & Weller (2011 WP 2011-001B survey), Neely & Weller (1999 WP 1999-016B intraday), Neely & Weller (2013 WP 2011-021D), Hsu/Taylor/Wang (2016 JIE working version), Famiglietti/Ivanova/Neely/Weller (2020 WP 2014-033I).

### Simple filter and moving-average rules genuinely did earn large risk-adjusted excess returns on major currencies from roughly 1973 to 1990. This was not data mining — it survived a true out-of-sample test run decades later.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed — published as Neely, Weller & Ulrich, 'The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis: Evidence from the Foreign Exchange Market', Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 44(2), 2009. I read the March 2007 working-paper version in full.

**Evidence:** Neely, Weller & Ulrich re-ran the exact fixed rules from five prior studies on data those authors never saw. Sweeney (1986) filter rules, original sample 1973:4-1980: 1% filter averaged 8.61% net annual return across 10 exchange rates; even the 0.5% filter averaged 5.45% net; average Sharpe for the 1% filter was 1.02 (avg s.e. 0.41). Uniform portfolio of all Sweeney filters: 5.11% net p.a., positive and statistically significant for all 10 currencies, average Sharpe 0.84. Levich & Thomas (1993) rules, original sample 1976-1990: 1% filter averaged 8.47% p.a. across four currencies; MA(5,20) 9.09%; MA(1,200) 8.33%; MA(1,5) 5.73%; average Sharpe 0.63. Taylor (1994) channel rules 1982-1990: 5.74% p.a. Neely/Weller/Dittmar genetic-programming rules 1981-1995: 2.94% p.a. average, Sharpe 0.36. For comparison the authors report 1974-2005:6 Sharpe ratios for MSCI total-return equity indices of 0.30 (Germany), 0.17 (Japan), 0.31 (UK), 0.37 (US). CAPM betas on the rule returns were almost all negative and small, so this is not equity beta in disguise.

**Against:** LeBaron (2002) found 150-day MA returns declined in the 1990s and explicitly speculated that data snooping was responsible for the earlier successes — i.e. that the 1970s-80s results were never real. NWU's out-of-sample design is the direct rebuttal and I weight it above LeBaron's conjecture, but the conjecture was serious and mainstream. Separately, Park & Irwin's survey of 95 modern studies found 20 negative and 19 mixed results alongside 56 positive, and cautions that most of the positive studies suffer from data snooping, ex post rule selection, and poor estimation of risk and transaction costs. The early literature was also not uniformly believed at the time: Neely & Weller record that academics generally dismissed Dooley & Shafer's findings and that the dominant view in the early 1980s was that technical profits must be wrong or sample-specific.

**Survives retail costs?** Yes, at institutional costs of that era, and the study is explicitly net. NWU model a one-way cost per position switch declining linearly from 10 basis points on 1 Jan 1973 to 1.88 bp on 30 Jun 2005 — this is more conservative than the flat 5bp used by most 1990s studies. All figures above are net of that. Cost context from the same papers: Frenkel & Levich estimated ~15bp round-trip, McCormick ~10bp, Sweeney used 12.5bp round-trip, Levich & Thomas computed 2.5bp per futures transaction for a large institution (4bp 'conservative'). Caveat that matters for you: these are proportional costs per position change only. No study models slippage on stops, weekend gaps, or retail swap/rollover markup, so every 'net' figure in this literature is an upper bound on what a retail account would have realised.

**Sources:** <https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf>, <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/adaptive-markets-hypothesis-evidence-from-the-foreign-exchange-market/9D336CDCA83233819EB5CDD0F4BC0DAA>, <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-6419.2007.00519.x>

### Those same rules stopped working. For filter and moving-average rules on major currencies the excess return went to approximately zero — and in several cases negative — by the early-to-mid 1990s. This is the single most robust finding in the field.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed — NWU (JFQA 2009) and Olson (Journal of Banking & Finance 28(1), 2004, 85-105). Famiglietti/Ivanova/Neely/Weller is a St. Louis Fed working paper (2014-033I, revised Sept 2020), so working-paper quality, read in full.

**Evidence:** Same NWU out-of-sample test, later period. Sweeney filter uniform portfolio: 5.11% net p.a. (1973-1980) fell to 2.92% (1981-2005:6); average Sharpe 0.84 fell to 0.41. Levich & Thomas rules are starker: the 1% filter went from +8.47% p.a. (1976-1990) to −1.14% p.a. (1991-2005:6), a swing of 9.61 percentage points; the uniform portfolio mean went to −0.11% p.a. and average Sharpe from 0.90 to −0.02; MA(1,5) earned positive returns for no currency and was −5.91% for CHF; no MA rule earned positive returns for more than two of four currencies; equal-means tests rejected for 21 of 36 cases. Taylor channel rules: 5.74% → 1.91%, none significantly positive. Taylor ARIMA: 7.09% → 2.97%, Sharpe 0.69 → 0.31. Genetic-programming rules: 2.94% → 0.15%, Sharpe 0.36 → 0.02. Fitted linear time trends: −21bp per year average for Sweeney rules (range −8bp CAD to −34bp GBP/CHF, significant 5 of 10 times), and −24 to −42bp per year for Levich-Thomas rules (significant 3 of 4). Andrews (1993) unknown-breakpoint tests place the break in the early 1990s. The authors' own summary: 'By the mid-1990s, profit opportunities had largely disappeared for these popular classes of rules.' Independently, Olson (2004) re-optimised MA rules on 18 developed currencies in successive 5-year windows from 1971-2000 and tested each out-of-sample in the next 5 years, finding returns fell from the 1970s to about zero by the 1990s. Famiglietti et al. (2020), extending the data to Dec 2012, describe 'the well-known dropoff in most portfolio returns after the early 1990s. Only the top-ranked portfolio remains profitable after 1993 or so.'

**Against:** The decline is weakly identified currency-by-currency, and NWU say so plainly. For the Sweeney rules they reject equal means in only 1 of 10 cases (GBP, 5% two-sided). The Schwartz criterion actually prefers a constant mean over a break or trend, reflecting its preference for parsimony. For channel, ARIMA, GP and Markov rules, time trends are mostly insignificant. The honest statement from the authors: 'the volatility of returns often prevents us from definitely concluding that mean trading rule returns have changed, although that is more likely than not' — exchange rates have roughly 10% annual standard deviation, so detecting a 2-3pp per annum drop needs very long samples. What carries the argument is the uniformity of the pattern across five independent studies, not any one test. Counter-evidence on the other side: Neely & Weller (2013) find that a dynamic strategy switching between rules and currencies shows profitability *returning* after 1997 — see the emerging-markets claim below for why that is not the good news it appears.

**Survives retail costs?** The question is moot for majors — the gross effect itself went to zero, so there is nothing left for costs to eat. Note the perverse detail: transaction costs *fell* over this period (about 10bp per position switch in the 1970s to about 2bp by the mid-2000s, per Neely & Weller), so the decline in net returns is understated relative to the decline in gross predictability. Cheaper trading did not save these rules; arguably cheaper trading is part of what arbitraged them away.

**Sources:** <https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378426602003990>, <https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2014/2014-033.pdf>, <https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2011/2011-001.pdf>

### Even the largest, most methodologically careful and most pro-technical-analysis modern study finds zero statistically significant predictive rules in developed-market currencies since the 1992-1996 period.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed — Hsu, Taylor & Wang, 'Technical trading: Is it still beating the foreign exchange market?', Journal of International Economics 102 (2016), 188-208. I read the full working version.

**Evidence:** Hsu, Taylor & Wang tested 21,195 technical rules on 30 currencies over 45 years (Jan 1971 - Sep 2015) using Hansen-style stepwise SPA testing to control data snooping. Their headline full-sample result is positive: 15 of 30 currencies predictable at 10%, best-rule annualised excess returns for developed currencies clustered 6.5% (DEM/EUR) to 7.7% (JPY), average 6.9% p.a.; emerging-market best rules averaged 9.5% p.a., up to 16.2% (RUB), 12.7% (KRW), 11.6% (COP). On the Sharpe criterion only 3 of 9 developed currencies were significant: NZD 0.652, DEM/EUR 0.681, JPY 0.752. But the sub-period table is the real finding. Splitting into eight sub-samples, they report: five of nine developed currencies predictable in 1972-1976, 'only a few predictive rules found in the 1980s, and none since the 1992-1996 period.' Their own words: 'technical predictability certainly used to be strong in developed country foreign exchange rates but has decayed in more recent decades', and they note the same downward trend in emerging markets. Their out-of-sample test (rules selected on pre-2012 data, evaluated Jan 2012 - Sep 2015) gives a developed-country portfolio Sharpe of 0.24 and 1.35% p.a., explicitly not statistically significant. The full-sample 6.9% figure is therefore an artefact of averaging a dead 25-year period with a live 20-year one.

**Against:** The authors themselves push back on their own null result: they warn it 'is possible that some profitable rules are not identified due to the low power of our tests with short sub-periods.' Their own footnote quantifies this brutally — with 3.7 years of out-of-sample data you would need a Sharpe of 0.85 or more to reach even 10% significance one-sided, and no developed-country rule achieved that in-sample or full-sample. So 'no significant rules since 1992' partly reflects test power, not proven absence. They also report break-even transaction costs far above estimated costs (developed-market break-even ratios of 6.6x to 43x actual costs on the return criterion, 10x to 100x on the Sharpe criterion) and conclude 'transaction costs do not necessarily eliminate the possibility of making significant profits.' That is a genuine pro-TA finding — but those break-even ratios are computed for the ex-post best-performing rules over the full 45-year sample, which is precisely the period-averaging problem above.

**Survives retail costs?** Their cost model is institutional: one-third of the quoted one-month forward bid-ask spread (they argue quoted spreads overstate what traders actually pay by ~3x), backfilled as 5bp one-way in the 1970s, 4bp in the 1980s, 3bp in the 1990s for developed currencies. Full-sample average one-way cost for developed currencies came out just under 4.5 basis points. All returns quoted are net of that. The developed-country out-of-sample result — 1.35% p.a., Sharpe 0.24, insignificant — is the number that matters for a retail trader in majors, and it is already net of costs more favourable than most retail accounts will see.

### The most recent large out-of-sample test finds the effect essentially gone: mean Sharpe falls from 0.66 in-sample to 0.06 out-of-sample over 1999-2020, returns do not survive modest transaction costs, and whatever abnormal return remains is fully explained by time-series momentum rather than by the rules themselves.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed — Research in International Business and Finance 63 (2022), article 101779. IMPORTANT LIMITATION: I could not retrieve the full text. Every host carrying it (ScienceDirect, SSRN, QUB, UCC, ADU repositories) was blocked by this session's network egress proxy. These figures come from abstract-level summaries returned by search, not from my own reading of the paper. Treat the 0.66/0.06 numbers as second-hand until verified against the article.

**Evidence:** Hutchinson, Kyziropoulos, O'Brien, O'Reilly & Sharma report that the mean Sharpe ratio of their sample portfolios fell from 0.66 in the in-sample period to 0.06 in an out-of-sample period running 1999 to 2020; that the returns do not survive modest transaction costs out-of-sample; and that time-series momentum is the single common factor driving returns across the full range of strategies, with any abnormal return generated by technical trading rules fully explained by it.

**Against:** I found no direct contradiction of this specific paper, which by the brief's own standard should make you suspicious rather than reassured — largely because I could not read it or its critics. The 'it's all just momentum' reduction does have a partial counterweight: Famiglietti, Ivanova, Neely & Weller (2020) tested CAPM, quadratic CAPM, downside-risk CAPM, Carhart 4-factor, C-CAPM, durable-consumption C-CAPM, the Lustig-Verdelhan carry factor, macroeconomic factors, and FX volatility/skewness/liquidity factors, and concluded 'No model plausibly accounts for much of the technical profitability.' They did not test time-series momentum as a factor, so the two findings are not strictly in conflict, but they point in different directions about whether rule returns are a known factor in disguise.

**Survives retail costs?** No — this is the paper's explicit finding. Returns do not survive modest transaction costs out-of-sample. A Sharpe of 0.06 is indistinguishable from zero before costs, and negative after them.

### At intraday frequency — the timeframe most retail FX systems actually trade — huge gross predictability exists and is completely destroyed by transaction costs. This is the cleanest 'costs decide FX' result in the literature.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed — Neely & Weller, Journal of International Money and Finance 22(2), 2003, 223-237; I read the full working-paper version (FRB St. Louis 1999-016B). Kozhan & Salmon (2010) is cited secondhand via the Neely & Weller 2011 survey, which I read in full.

**Evidence:** Neely & Weller trained genetic programs on half-hourly CHF, DEM, JPY and GBP against USD for 1996. Rules trained with zero transaction costs produced out-of-sample returns 'in three of the four cases over 100 per cent per annum' — strong evidence of a genuinely predictable component. But those rules trade roughly once an hour, so the BREAK-EVEN one-way transaction cost was at most 1.01 basis points (GBP, the best case). Their own estimate of realistic institutional one-way cost is 2-2.5 basis points. So the rules lose money for a large institution, let alone anyone else. When they retrained under the constraint that positions may only change during the 12 most liquid hours of the weekday (DEM 0600-1800 GMT, JPY 0400-1600, CHF 0500-1700, GBP 0500-1700), break-even costs fell 'below that which an institutional trader would face' in essentially every case. Their conclusion on the linear-model variant is categorical: 'the apparent profitability of the trading rules obtained with c = 0.0002 is solely attributable to trading during periods of reduced market activity when transaction costs are likely to be substantially higher than the benchmark figure of 2.5 basis points that we have chosen.' In other words the apparent intraday edge was an artefact of backtesting through hours when nobody could actually trade at the assumed spread. Published abstract: 'When realistic transaction costs and trading hours are taken into account, we find no evidence of excess returns to the trading rules derived with either methodology.' Consistent later evidence: Kozhan & Salmon (2010), using tick-by-tick GBP/USD from Reuters D3000, found a genetic-algorithm rule earned significant profits net of costs in 2003 but that those profits had disappeared by 2008.

**Against:** Hsu, Taylor & Wang (2016) argue the opposite conclusion for daily rules, reporting break-even costs 6x to 100x above estimated costs and stating that 'the best-performing technical trading rules do not rely on frequent trading for developed country currencies.' The reconciliation is trading frequency, not disagreement: their best developed-market rules fired between 24 and 917 times across 11,660 trading days, versus roughly once an hour for Neely & Weller's intraday rules. Low-frequency rules have wide cost tolerance; high-frequency rules have none. Also note Dempster & Leemans (2006) reported a 26% per annum gross return on one-minute EUR/USD data from Jan 2000 to Jan 2002 — but Neely & Weller flag that as a gross figure.

**Survives retail costs?** Emphatically no at intraday frequency. Break-even 1.01bp one-way versus 2-2.5bp realistic institutional cost. Retail is worse still: you pay spread plus commission plus swap/rollover markup plus slippage, and none of the last three appear anywhere in these cost models. The direct implication for your system: trading frequency is the variable that decides whether any FX edge survives, and it dominates win rate entirely.

**Sources:** <https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/1999/1999-016.pdf>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261560602001018>, <https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2011/2011-001.pdf>

### Data-snooping corrections wipe out the large majority of apparent FX rule profitability, and the damage is worst in the most recent data and in emerging markets — exactly where naive backtests look most exciting.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed — Qi & Wu, Journal of Money, Credit and Banking 38(8), 2006; Kuang, Schröder & Wang, International Journal of Forecasting 30(2), 2014; Coakley, Marzano & Nankervis, International Review of Financial Analysis 45 (2016), 273-282. LIMITATION: all three full texts were behind hosts blocked by this session's egress proxy (SSRN, ScienceDirect, St Andrews, Essex, IDEAS). Qi & Wu's result I have from Neely & Weller's 2011 survey, which I read in full and which characterises it directly; the other two are from abstract-level search summaries. I did not verify these numbers against the articles themselves.

**Evidence:** Three layers. (1) Qi & Wu (2006) were first to apply White's (2000) Reality Check in FX, across 2,127 rules: profitability survives for the full 1973-1998 sample, but is 'considerably weaker' for the 1986-1998 sub-sample, and they report that the data-snooping problem is more serious in the second half of the sample, concluding the FX market became more efficient over time. (2) Kuang, Schröder & Wang tested 25,988 trading strategies on ten emerging FX markets. Standard tests identify hundreds to thousands of significant profitable strategies, with the best rules generating annual mean excess returns above 30%. Applying Hansen's StepM and the SSPA test of Hsu, Hsu & Kuan — which account for the entire search process — 'almost all of these profits vanish.' Their title is the finding: the profitability is illusory. (3) Coakley, Marzano & Nankervis examined 113,148 rules on 22 currencies over 1996:10-2015:06: before controlling for data snooping the rules achieved profits of up to 29.7% per annum; after accounting for both data-snooping bias and transaction costs, the rules are profitable only in isolated one-to-three-month windows and not over the full sample, with positive Sharpe ratios routinely offset by negative ones.

**Against:** Hsu, Taylor & Wang (2016) is the serious counterweight and is the strongest paper of the group: applying a stepwise SPA test to 21,195 rules, they still find significant predictability survives correction for 15 of 30 currencies over the full sample, and explicitly note their developed-currency results are 'largely consistent with the empirical findings of Qi and Wu (2006)'. So snooping correction does not zero everything out — it zeroes out the recent and the developed-market subsets. Also genuinely against the pure-snooping story: Neely, Weller & Ulrich's out-of-sample replication shows the original 1970s-80s profits were real, which no amount of snooping correction can explain away.

**Survives retail costs?** Partly, and inconsistently. Coakley et al. is the most directly relevant because it applies both corrections jointly and covers 1996-2015: after data snooping AND transaction costs, profitability exists only in scattered one-to-three-month windows, not over the full sample. That is the profile of noise, not of an edge you can build a system on. The Kuang et al. >30% p.a. figures are pre-correction and should be read as a demonstration of how large a purely spurious backtest result can be — 25,988 strategies will always produce spectacular winners by chance.

**Sources:** <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=788013>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207013000964>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521916300400>, <https://repository.essex.ac.uk/16362/>, <https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2011/2011-001.pdf>, <https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf>

### Whatever technical edge still existed after 2000 lived almost entirely in emerging-market currencies — which carry 2-4x wider spreads, are often only accessible via non-deliverable forwards, and are the currencies a retail FX account can least realistically trade.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed for Hsu/Taylor/Wang (JIE 2016). Neely & Weller (2013) 'Lessons from the Evolution of Foreign Exchange Trading Strategies' is a St. Louis Fed working paper (2011-021D) published in the Journal of Banking & Finance; Famiglietti et al. is a working paper (2014-033I). I read all three in full.

**Evidence:** Neely & Weller (2013) ran an ex-ante rule-selection strategy over 1975-2012: the best ex-ante strategy earned 10.08% gross, 9.40% net after 13.76 trades a year, Sharpe 0.78. But when they re-ran the entire selection exercise using ONLY developed-country currencies, 'about half of the 10-strategy and 50-strategy portfolios earn negative excess returns in the final sample (2000-2012) and none of the portfolios earn statistically significant positive Sharpe ratios.' Hsu, Taylor & Wang's out-of-sample test (2012-2015) tells the same story with different data: developed-country portfolio 1.35% p.a., Sharpe 0.24, insignificant; emerging-market portfolio 7.3% p.a., Sharpe 1.81, t-ratio 3.5. Famiglietti et al. (2020), ranking individual rules by ex-post Sharpe over the full sample, find the top six are all emerging: RUB filter .005 (9.40% p.a., Sharpe 1.19, 2000-2012), RUB MA(1,5) (7.15%, 0.93), CLP channel (8.11%, 0.88), TWD MA(5,20) (4.25%, 0.87), TWD channel (4.02%, 0.85), TWD mom(60) (3.74%, 0.83). The cost asymmetry is documented in the same literature: Burnside et al. (2007) found bid-ask spreads for emerging-market currencies over 1997-2006 were between two and four times as large as developed-market spreads, and Neely & Weller warn explicitly that 'using the same transaction cost for all currencies will exaggerate the relative profitability of trading in emerging market currencies.'

**Against:** Hsu, Taylor & Wang find the decay trend is present in emerging markets too — 'the predictability of technical trading rules in emerging markets also decreases over time' — so this is a shrinking refuge, not a stable one. More damagingly, Kuang, Schröder & Wang's 'Illusory Profitability' paper is aimed squarely at exactly this claim: testing 25,988 strategies on ten emerging FX markets, they find almost all apparent profits vanish under StepM/SSPA correction. The emerging-market result is therefore the single most contested finding in the modern literature, with Hsu/Taylor/Wang and Kuang et al. reaching opposite conclusions using similar snooping-correction machinery on overlapping currency sets.

**Survives retail costs?** This is the crux and the answer is probably no for a retail trader, even where it survives for an institution. Hsu/Taylor/Wang's emerging-market Sharpe of 1.81 is net of costs estimated as one-third of quoted one-month forward spreads — a discount they justify by institutional execution. A retail account pays something closer to the full quoted spread or worse on RUB, CLP, COP, TWD, PHP, RON and TRY, i.e. roughly 3x the modelled cost, on currencies with 2-4x the developed-market spread to begin with. Neely & Weller also flag that for Russia, Brazil, Peru, Chile and Taiwan only non-deliverable forwards were available. Multiply the modelled cost by three and most of these results go away.

### The classic chart pattern — head-and-shoulders — has been tested in FX and found profitable but DOMINATED by simpler mechanical rules. No other discretionary chart pattern has a serious cost-adjusted FX literature at all.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed for Chang & Osler, Economic Journal 109(458), 1999, 636-661; Osler & Chang (1995) is a Federal Reserve Bank of New York staff report, i.e. working-paper quality. LIMITATION: full texts of both were on hosts blocked by this session's egress proxy (newyorkfed.org, Wiley, reversal-patterns.technicalanalysis.org.uk, biz.uiowa.edu). I have Chang & Osler's finding from the published abstract and from Neely & Weller's full-text characterisation; I could not extract their actual return numbers, bootstrap p-values, or cost assumptions.

**Evidence:** Osler & Chang (1995, FRB New York Staff Report 4, 'Head and Shoulders: Not Just a Flaky Pattern') built a computer-implemented algorithm to identify head-and-shoulders patterns from criteria in published technical-analysis manuals, applied it to daily major-currency rates from March 1973 to June 1994, and compared profits against 10,000 bootstrapped random-walk series. They found predictive power for some currencies. Chang & Osler (1999, Economic Journal) extended this with two rationality criteria, profitability and efficiency, and reached the decisive result: the rule is profitable, but NOT efficient, because it is dominated by simpler trading rules. Neely & Weller's survey summarises the FX evidence as Osler & Chang finding 'some value in this classic pattern for some currencies' — a notably weaker characterisation than the pattern's reputation. On the wider pattern literature: Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000) developed the kernel-regression smoothing approach for identifying head-and-shoulders and other patterns, and Savin, Weller & Zvingelis (2007) found significant predictive power for stock returns up to three months — but Neely & Weller state flatly that 'the kernel mean regression methodology has yet to be applied to the foreign exchange market.' That remained true as of their 2011 survey and I found nothing filling the gap since.

**Against:** The two papers by the same authors sit in tension with each other by design — the 1995 paper establishes the pattern beats a random walk, the 1999 paper establishes it loses to a moving-average rule. For your purposes the 1999 result is the operative one and it contradicts the whole premise of pattern-hunting: if the famous pattern is dominated by a simple MA crossover, the pattern is not the source of the edge. Both studies also end in 1994, i.e. they cover precisely the era that every other paper in this review shows was subsequently arbitraged away, and neither has been re-run out-of-sample on post-1994 data as far as I can find.

**Survives retail costs?** Unknown, and that is itself the answer. I could not retrieve either paper's transaction-cost treatment. Chang & Osler's efficiency finding makes the cost question largely academic: a rule that is dominated by a cheaper, lower-frequency alternative before costs is dominated by more after them, since pattern rules require discretionary identification and typically tighter stops. Note also that head-and-shoulders is the ONLY FX chart pattern with peer-reviewed testing I could locate. Triangles, flags, pennants, double tops, wedges and the rest have no cost-adjusted, snooping-corrected FX literature I could find.

**Sources:** <https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr4.html>, <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1468-0297.00466>, <https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/2011/2011-001.pdf>

### The early FX technical profits were NOT compensation for risk. Roughly a dozen asset-pricing models fail to explain them — which paradoxically strengthens the decay finding rather than supporting technical trading.

**Quality:** working paper — FRB St. Louis WP 2014-033I (Famiglietti, Ivanova, Neely & Weller, revised September 2020), read in full. The supporting NWU material is peer-reviewed (JFQA 2009).

**Evidence:** Famiglietti, Ivanova, Neely & Weller tested CAPM, quadratic CAPM, downside-risk CAPM, Carhart's 4-factor model, the C-CAPM, an extended C-CAPM with durable consumption, the Lustig-Verdelhan carry-trade factor model, and models including macroeconomic factors plus FX volatility, skewness and liquidity. Their conclusion: 'No model plausibly accounts for much of the technical profitability. This failure implicitly supports non-risk based explanations such as adaptive markets.' Their 12 ex-ante ranked portfolios over 1973-2012 show excess returns declining monotonically from 4.41% p.a. (p1) to 0.69% p.a. (p12), with ex-post Sharpe ratios from 0.81 (p1) to 0.17 (p12); daily returns show little skewness, with skewness increasing at monthly and quarterly horizons and heavy excess kurtosis at the daily frequency. Earlier, NWU found CAPM betas on rule returns were almost all negative and small, robust to using German or UK equity indices. Neely & Weller's survey draws the logical consequence: a believer in efficient markets 'may argue that such tests have omitted some unknown risk factor. That argument, however, is substantially refuted by the decline in the profitability of simple rules over time, essentially to zero.' A risk premium does not vanish because people discover it; an inefficiency does.

**Against:** Hutchinson et al. (2022) report the opposite kind of result — that abnormal returns from technical rules are fully explained by time-series momentum, a known factor. Famiglietti et al. did not include a time-series momentum factor among the dozen they tested, so the two are not formally contradictory, but if Hutchinson et al. are right then technical rule returns ARE a factor exposure and the 'no risk model explains it' conclusion is incomplete. Also relevant: Famiglietti et al. note their own Figure 3 shows no obvious decline in technical returns during the 1994-95 peso crisis, the 1997-98 Asian crisis or the 2007-08 financial crisis — 'if anything... a temporary upward spike in technical returns during 2008-2009' — which cuts against a crash-risk-premium story for these returns specifically.

**Survives retail costs?** All Famiglietti et al. figures are net, using the Neely & Weller (2013) cost model: 5bp one-way for advanced countries in the 1970s, 4bp in the 1980s, 3bp in the 1990s, then one-third of quoted one-month forward spreads once Bloomberg data begins Dec 1995, with a 1bp floor for all currencies at all times. The relevance for you is inverted from what it looks like: the finding that these are not risk premia means the returns were genuine free money — and genuine free money is exactly the kind that gets competed away, which is what the data show happened after 1993.

### Among actual retail currency traders, using technical indicators is NEGATIVELY associated with performance.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed — Abbey & Doukas, 'Is Technical Analysis Profitable for Individual Currency Traders?', Journal of Portfolio Management 39(1), Fall 2012, 142-150. LIMITATION: the full text host (efmaefm.org) and the journal were blocked by this session's egress proxy; I have the finding from the published abstract via search, not from my own reading, and I could not extract regression coefficients or effect magnitudes.

**Evidence:** Abbey & Doukas studied a proprietary database of 428 individual currency traders from an online advisory service covering March 2004 to September 2009, testing whether use of RSI, Bollinger Bands, MACD and the 8/18-day moving-average crossover was associated with performance. Their result: the use of technical analysis by individual currency traders is negatively associated with performance, and specifically, technical currency traders who use Bollinger Bands underperform relative to peers who do not use it.

**Against:** The same authors, using the same 428-account dataset, published 'Do individual currency traders make money?' (Journal of International Money and Finance 56, 2015, 158-177), reporting that these currency traders earned positive abnormal returns even after accounting for transaction costs, and that day traders traded more frequently AND outperformed non-day traders on raw, passive-benchmark and risk-adjusted returns. So this is not a simple 'retail always loses' dataset — it is a dataset in which the traders did fine overall but the ones leaning on technical indicators did worse. There is also an obvious selection concern I cannot rule out: 428 accounts from a paid online advisory service are not a random sample of retail FX, and survivorship in such a sample would bias the 2015 positive-returns result upward.

**Survives retail costs?** The 2015 companion paper states its positive abnormal returns are after transaction costs; the 2012 technical-analysis result is a relative, cross-sectional finding (TA users versus non-users within the same cost environment), so costs do not confound the sign. The practical reading: within a population of real retail FX accounts paying real retail costs, the indicator-driven subgroup did worse than the rest.

### Central bank intervention does not create technical trading profits — closing off the main 'structural reason the edge should persist' argument.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed — LeBaron, Journal of International Economics 49(1), 1999; Neely (2002), Journal of International Economics. I read the full-text reproduction and discussion of both in Neely & Weller's 2011 survey, and the corroborating analysis in Hsu/Taylor/Wang's full text.

**Evidence:** LeBaron (1999) showed a striking correlation between US intervention days and MA rule profitability: removing intervention-day returns left the mean technical return statistically insignificant. Neely & Weller reproduce it with a MA(1,150) rule on daily DEM/USD and JPY/USD, 1979-1992: gross annual returns of 6.97% (DEM) and 8.35% (JPY), both significant, falling to 2.07% and 3.53% and losing significance when intervention days are removed, with Markov p-values of 8% and 5% for the chance that random removal would do as much damage. On its face, intervention creates the returns. But Neely (2002), using higher-frequency data and intervention series from four monetary authorities, showed almost all of the trading rule returns on intervention days accrued OVERNIGHT, before intervention could plausibly have occurred — the DEM overnight return fell from 7.5% to 4.5% and the JPY from 7.84% to 4.15% when intervention days were removed, i.e. the damage lands before the event. Causation runs the other way: central banks lean against strong trends, and technical rules happen to be positioned in those trends. Hsu, Taylor & Wang add independent confirmation from a different angle — their sub-period analysis finds no particularly strong predictability during periods of intensive central bank intervention such as the global financial crisis.

**Against:** Szakmary & Mathur (1997) found results similar to LeBaron's using monthly data on an international dataset, so the raw intervention correlation replicates across samples — it is the causal interpretation, not the correlation, that Neely (2002) overturns. Silber (1994) argued the relative uselessness of technical rules in equity and commodity markets, where there is no intervention, buttresses the intervention story. I am reporting the resolution favoured by the surveying authors, who are also the authors of the rebuttal paper, which is a conflict of interest worth noting; but the overnight-timing evidence is mechanically hard to argue with.

**Survives retail costs?** The LeBaron/Neely figures quoted are GROSS annual returns, explicitly labelled as such in Neely & Weller's Table III — do not read 6.97% or 8.35% as achievable net returns. The relevance to your system is structural rather than numerical: one popular justification for expecting an FX technical edge to persist is that central banks are non-profit-maximising participants who reliably hand money to trend-followers. The evidence says that mechanism is not operating. Remove it and there is no identified reason for a surviving edge to be durable.

### Averaged across rules and currencies without ex-post selection, FX technical trading produces a Sharpe ratio of about 0.17 — the headline numbers in this literature are all selection-conditioned.

**Quality:** working paper (FRB St. Louis 2011-021D, published in Journal of Banking & Finance) and working paper (2014-033I); both read in full.

**Evidence:** Neely & Weller (2013) built an equally weighted portfolio of 17 bilateral rules for each currency over all available data, 1975-2012. Result: 'For most currencies the net annual returns are modestly positive—in the range of 0 to 5%—but eight are negative. About a quarter of the exchange rates produce statistically significant positive net returns. The mean Sharpe ratio over all rules and exchange rates is 0.17.' Their much-quoted 9.40% net / Sharpe 0.78 figure is the rank-1 strategy out of a pool of up to 680 rule-currency pairs, re-ranked monthly. Famiglietti et al.'s 12 ranked portfolios show the same gradient — 4.41% p.a. and Sharpe 0.81 at rank 1, decaying to 0.69% p.a. and Sharpe 0.17 at rank 12 — and note that after 1993 only the top-ranked portfolio stays profitable at all. The pattern is consistent across every paper in this review: an impressive number is always the maximum over a large search, and the average over the search is near zero.

**Against:** In fairness to the selection procedures, Neely & Weller's ranking is genuinely ex ante — rules are ranked on past Sharpe only, and the out-of-sample month that follows is what is measured, so a trader could actually have implemented it. They also show the ranking works: higher-ranked strategies reliably deliver better net returns, which would not happen if ranking were pure noise, and the top-5 portfolio achieved an average Sharpe of 0.78 across 1997-2012 with positive Sharpe in 12 of 16 years. Diversification is real too — the top-5 individual strategies had annual standard deviations of 9.79% to 11.42% but the portfolio's was 6.91%. So this is not a pure data-mining artefact. But note that the post-1997 recovery driving that 0.78 disappears when emerging-market currencies are excluded.

**Survives retail costs?** The 0.17 average Sharpe is already net of the Neely & Weller cost model (5/4/3bp one-way for advanced countries by decade, one-third of quoted forward spreads thereafter, 1bp floor). A Sharpe of 0.17 with a 10% annual standard deviation is roughly 1.7% per annum before any retail cost premium, slippage, or swap markup — well inside the noise, and it would not survive a retail cost structure. This is the number to anchor on when someone shows you a backtest: the honest expected value of picking FX technical rules without ex-post hindsight is approximately zero.

## The FX factor literature — carry, momentum, value (PPP), the dollar factor, and the FX volatility risk premium — assessed for Sharpe ratios, drawdowns, crash risk, risk-based vs behavioural explanations, survival after realistic retail costs, and data-snooping robustness. Framing note for the trader: this literature almost never reports a "win rate," because in FX the hit rate is not the variable that determines whether you make money. Carry is the canonical high-win-rate, negative-skew trade — it wins most months and gives it all back in a few weeks. That is precisely the failure mode the "highest win rate pattern" request walks into.

### The FX carry trade (long high-interest-rate currencies, short low-interest-rate currencies) has earned a positive average excess return in most academic samples, with annualized Sharpe ratios roughly in the 0.4-0.9 range depending on construction, currency universe and period — i.e. broadly equity-like, not spectacular.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JF, JFE, JFQA, NBER Macro Annual, Annual Review of Financial Economics) — but read via search summaries, not source tables

**Evidence:** Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen (NBER Macro Annual 2008) report annualized Sharpe ratios of 0.44 (weekly data) and 0.41 (quarterly) for their carry portfolio. Burnside, Eichenbaum & Rebelo (Annual Review of Financial Economics 2011) report an average Sharpe of 0.42 for INDIVIDUAL currency carry trades and 'more than twice as large' (~0.9) for the equally-weighted diversified basket — diversification, not the trade itself, is where the Sharpe comes from; Burnside et al. (JEEA 2008) put the diversification gain at 'over 50%'. Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf (Journal of Finance 2012) report a long-short quintile carry portfolio earning 'more than 5% per annum even after accounting for transaction costs' over 48 currencies. Jurek (JFE 2014) reports G10 carry Sharpe ratios 'equal to or greater than those of equity market factors' over 1990-2012. Accominotti, Cen, Chambers & Marsh (JFQA 2019) estimate a Sharpe of 0.55 over the last century, but ONLY in floating-rate regimes. NOTE: all figures via search-engine summaries; I could not open the tables.

**Against:** Substantial and directly opposed. (1) Doskov & Swinkels (Journal of International Money and Finance 2015) extend to 1900-2012 and find a Sharpe of only 0.26 (2.4% p.a.) over 112 years — 'markedly lower than the Sharpe ratios above 0.6 reported for recent sample periods.' The modern high-Sharpe samples look like a favourable slice. (2) Accominotti et al. (JFQA 2019) find carry returns were ZERO in fixed-exchange-rate regimes — the premium is regime-conditional, not a law of nature. (3) The post-2008 decline paper (Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money, 2021/2022) finds that for a carry portfolio levered to equity-like volatility, performance dropped 5.4% PER YEAR between pre- and post-GFC periods — 'on par with the average market risk premium in the US.' (4) The only pure-play retail G10 carry vehicle in the US, Invesco DB G10 Currency Harvest (DBV), was liquidated in March 2023 for lack of assets after ~16.5 years; an industry writeup of the underlying DB G10 Currency Future Harvest index describes it as producing 'nothing' over nineteen and a half years while taking a 37% drawdown with persistent negative skew. A factor that a real, live, low-fee retail product could not monetize over 16 years is the single strongest contradiction available.

**Survives retail costs?** PARTIALLY at institutional cost levels, DOUBTFUL at retail cost levels. Institutional: Menkhoff et al. (JF 2012) say >5% p.a. survives quoted bid-ask spreads; Burnside et al. (JEEA 2008) say 'large positive Sharpe ratios emerge when the trading strategy takes bid-ask spreads into account.' But Filippou, Maurer, Pezzo & Taylor (JFE 2024, vol 159) show quoted-spread costs are small while VOLUME PRICE IMPACT is 'sizable and quickly erodes returns, leaving many popular strategies unprofitable' once a fund reaches size — carry named explicitly. Retail: the binding cost is the swap/rollover markup, not the spread. IG publishes its FX overnight funding as tom-next plus an admin fee of 0.3% p.a. on standard CFDs and 0.8% p.a. on mini contracts. A DBV-style basket runs ~200% gross notional (long 3, short 3), so the admin fee alone costs roughly 0.6%-1.6% of gross notional per year (my arithmetic, not a sourced figure) — against a G10 top-3-minus-bottom-3 yield spread that has frequently been only ~1.5-3.5% p.a. in the post-GFC era (model-knowledge-only). That is plausibly 20-60% of the entire gross carry consumed by one broker fee, before spreads and before the EM legs where retail swap markups and spreads are far worse — and the EM legs are exactly where the academic carry premium is concentrated.

### Carry is the textbook high-win-rate / catastrophic-payoff-ratio trade: returns are strongly negatively skewed and fat-tailed, and the losses arrive in violent clusters. This is the single most important fact for a trader who is optimizing for win rate.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (NBER Macro Annual, Critical Finance Review) plus central-bank publication (Bank of England) for 1998; the August 2024 figures are industry/press quality only

**Evidence:** Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen document that carry returns are negatively skewed, that the skewness arises from an ASYMMETRIC amplification mechanism (losses force levered speculators into funding constraints, they unwind, which depresses prices further, raising volatility and margins — gains are not amplified), and critically that 'in times when the interest rate differential is high, and therefore carry trades look particularly attractive in terms of conditional mean return, the skewness of carry trade returns is also particularly negative.' The trade looks best exactly when it is most dangerous. Daniel, Hodrick & Lu (Critical Finance Review 2017, 6:211-262) report for G10 developed-market carry drawdowns ranging from -7.2% over 30 days to -32% over 399 days (worst: late-July 2007 to early-February 2009); for a combined developed+EM carry, -9.0% over 18 days to -23% over 130 days (early-Aug 2008 to early-Feb 2009). They also report that portfolios of carry trades are MORE skewed and heavier-tailed than the individual pairs, that skewness point estimates are negative for every base currency except EUR with high significance for JPY, NOK, SEK, CHF, NZD and AUD, and that every strategy shows positive, significant excess kurtosis. Crash episodes: the yen appreciated ~14% on 6-8 October 1998, its largest two-day move since floating began in 1973, with carry unwinding explicitly named as the amplifier (Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin 1999). In August 2024, G10, EM and global carry baskets fell roughly 10% from May, at about twice the usual pace of carry drawdowns, with the 5 August cross-asset shock the largest single-day move since March 2020.

**Against:** Two important qualifiers, both of which cut AGAINST the simple 'carry crashes are the risk premium' story rather than against the crash itself. (1) Daniel, Hodrick & Lu find the negative skew is specific to DOLLAR-NEUTRAL carry: a diversified DOLLAR-carry portfolio has a higher average return, higher Sharpe, MINIMAL skewness, is uncorrelated with standard risk factors and shows no downside risk. That is a real finding but should raise suspicion, not confidence — a strategy with a high Sharpe, no skew and no factor exposure is usually either a small-sample artefact or an unpriced risk not yet identified. (2) Jurek (JFE 2014) buys crash protection with out-of-the-money FX options and finds the crash-hedged carry trade KEEPS most of its return: crash risk premia account for AT MOST one-third (elsewhere 30-40%) of the carry excess return. So crash risk does not explain carry, and conversely, hedging the crash does not destroy the premium — which undercuts the clean 'you're paid for tail risk' narrative in both directions.

**Survives retail costs?** The skew survives costs and gets WORSE with retail leverage. The mechanism Brunnermeier/Nagel/Pedersen describe is a funding-constraint spiral — margin calls forcing liquidation. A retail trader at 30:1 (ESMA major-FX cap) or 50:1 (US cap) is the most fragile participant in that spiral and gets stopped out at the worst prices. Retail stop-outs and slippage during a 14%-in-two-days move are not modelled anywhere in this literature; academic returns assume you survive the drawdown. A -32% G10 carry drawdown at 10:1 leverage is a total loss of capital several times over. No source needed for that arithmetic; it is the reason win rate is the wrong objective.

**Sources:** <https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/593088>, <https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/6378/Daniel.Hodrick.Lu.Carry%20Trade.Critical%20Finance%20Review.2017.pdf>, <https://doi.org/10.1561/104.00000051>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X14001081>, <https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1999/the-yen-dollar-exchange-rate-in-1998-views-from-options-markets.pdf>, <https://theconversation.com/a-carry-crash-also-kicked-off-the-global-financial-crisis-17-years-ago-heres-why-its-unlikely-to-get-as-bad-this-time>

### Currency momentum (sorting on past 1-12 month currency returns) produces a large gross cross-sectional spread — up to 10% p.a. — but is the factor most thoroughly destroyed by transaction costs, and what remains sits in currencies a retail trader should not be trading.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JFE) — read via search summaries, not source tables; the '6-10% gross vs 4% net' split comes from a practitioner summary site (CXO Advisory) and is lower quality

**Evidence:** Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf (JFE 2012, 106(3):660-684) document a cross-sectional spread in excess returns of up to 10% per annum between past winner and loser currencies across 48 currencies, 1976-2010, and report a Sharpe ratio of 0.82 for their benchmark strategy. They state explicitly that 'adjusting returns for bid-ask spreads lowers the profitability of momentum strategies significantly since momentum portfolios are skewed towards currencies with high transaction costs,' and that 'there seem to be very effective limits to arbitrage that prevent momentum returns from being easily exploitable.' A secondary summary of the same paper puts it at 6-10% annualized gross narrowing to about 4% net for the best strategy. The spread is NOT explained by traditional risk factors and behaves consistently with investor under- and overreaction — i.e. the explanation is behavioural, and the reason it persists is that it is expensive to trade.

**Against:** Strong. (1) The authors' own finding that momentum concentrates in high-transaction-cost currencies is a self-contradiction of tradability — the return is a payment for bearing illiquidity, and retail pays a worse price for that illiquidity than the study assumes. (2) Filippou, Maurer, Pezzo & Taylor (JFE 2024) find that once volume price impact is modelled, momentum (named alongside carry and mean-variance-optimized FX portfolios) 'turns unprofitable' past a certain fund size. (3) Barroso & Santa-Clara (JFQA 2015) find momentum DOES contribute to an optimal currency portfolio out-of-sample — so the evidence is genuinely split, and the split falls along the line of who is modelling costs how. (4) I found no study showing G10-only currency momentum (the only universe a retail trader can trade at reasonable cost) surviving costs with an attractive Sharpe; a Bayes Business School working paper titled 'Momentum Effects: G10 Currency Return Survivals' exists but I could not open it.

**Survives retail costs?** LARGELY NO at retail. Academic net-of-spread figures (~4% p.a. best case) assume interbank bid-ask on forwards, and the return is concentrated in EM/exotic currencies. Retail spreads on TRY, ZAR, MXN, BRL are multiples of interbank, retail swap markups on those legs are worse still, and monthly rebalancing of a long-short basket means the cost is paid 24+ times a year across legs. A 4% gross-of-retail-costs edge does not survive that. Specific retail cost numbers for exotic pairs: not found in any credible source.

**Sources:** <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X12001353>, <https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v106y2012i3p660-684.html>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X24001090>, <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/beyond-the-carry-trade-optimal-currency-portfolios/CBE64E63B417A8BB12AF95FBD1AC105D>, <https://www.cxoadvisory.com/momentum-investing/momentum-investing-for-currencies/>, <https://www.bayes.citystgeorges.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0006/368988/G10-momentum-paper.pdf>

### Currency value (real-exchange-rate / PPP deviation) is the WEAKEST of the major FX factors in raw form, and only becomes convincing after adjusting the real exchange rate for country fundamentals — an adjustment a retail trader cannot realistically replicate.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (RFS, JF) — read via search summaries only; I have NO currency-specific Sharpe ratio for value from any source I could verify

**Evidence:** Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf, 'Currency Value' (Review of Financial Studies 2017, 30(2):416-441) find that raw real exchange rates DO have predictive power for the cross-section of currency excess returns, but that 'adjusting real exchange rates for key country-specific fundamentals (productivity, the quality of export goods, net foreign assets, and output gaps) better isolates information related to the currency risk premium, and the resultant measure of currency value displays considerably stronger predictive power.' The clean reading: raw PPP is a weak signal; the strong signal requires four fundamental data series per country, correctly lagged and vintage-matched. Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (Journal of Finance 2013) include 10 currency pairs vs USD in their value-and-momentum study and find value and momentum premia are consistent across all eight asset classes with a strong common factor structure — but I could not open their tables to extract the currency-specific Sharpe ratios.

**Against:** Directly and from a strong source. Barroso & Santa-Clara (JFQA 2015, 50(5)), in a 16-year out-of-sample test of optimal currency portfolios, find that 'carry, momentum and reversal all contribute to portfolio performance, whereas THE REAL EXCHANGE RATE AND THE CURRENT ACCOUNT DO NOT.' That is a direct out-of-sample rejection of the raw PPP-value signal in the exact test that matters (does adding it improve a portfolio out-of-sample?). Reconciling this with Menkhoff et al. 2017 is straightforward and unflattering: raw value adds nothing; only the heavily fundamentals-adjusted version works, which is another way of saying the signal is fragile to specification — a classic data-snooping warning sign. Also note PPP mean-reversion half-lives are conventionally estimated at 3-5 years (model-knowledge-only, I could not source this), which makes value a multi-year holding-period bet, not a chart pattern.

**Survives retail costs?** Probably YES on cost grounds, NO on everything else. Value has the lowest turnover of any FX factor (annual or slower rebalancing), so spread costs are close to irrelevant. But it holds positions for years, meaning years of accumulated swap/rollover — and if the value signal points you long a low-yield currency (which it frequently does, since overvaluation and low yield are correlated), you pay negative carry the whole time PLUS the broker's 0.3-0.8% p.a. admin fee. The cost that kills value at retail is financing, not execution. No source quantifies this; it is my arithmetic on the IG fee schedule.

### The dollar factor is real and is arguably the most important single factor in FX — two factors (a carry factor and a dollar factor) account for 20-90% of exchange rate MOVEMENTS, not just returns — and the 'dollar carry trade' (timing aggregate dollar exposure on the average forward discount) has historically delivered a HIGHER Sharpe than the standard cross-sectional carry trade.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JF, JFE, QJE, Critical Finance Review) — read via search summaries only, no tables verified

**Evidence:** Verdelhan (Journal of Finance 2018, 73(1):375-418) shows a carry factor prices the interest-rate-sorted cross-section and a dollar factor prices a novel dollar-beta-sorted cross-section, and that together 'these two factors account for 20% to 90% of the daily, monthly, quarterly, and annual exchange rate movements in developed countries and in emerging and developing countries with floating exchange rates' (18-80% for the two high-minus-low factors at monthly frequency). Lustig, Roussanov & Verdelhan (JFE 2014, 111(3)) introduce the 'dollar carry trade,' which 'delivers large excess returns, uncorrelated with the returns on well-known carry trade strategies' and 'offers even higher Sharpe ratios compared to the high-minus-low global currency carry trade'; $100 invested Dec 1983 grows to $1,467 by end of sample, of which $860 is the interest-rate component and $607 predictable dollar moves. Daniel, Hodrick & Lu (CFR 2017) independently corroborate that the diversified dollar-carry portfolio beats dollar-neutral carry on Sharpe with minimal skewness. Hassan & Mano (QJE 2019, 134(1):397-450) decompose UIP violations and find the forward premium puzzle and the 'dollar trade' are 'intimately linked, both driven almost exclusively by the cross-time component,' while the 'carry trade' anomaly is driven largely by CROSS-SECTIONAL UIP violations — the dollar factor and the carry factor are economically distinct phenomena, not two views of one thing.

**Against:** Several real problems. (1) The dollar carry trade is a SINGLE TIMING BET — one position, on or off, conditioned on the average forward discount. Its entire track record is a handful of independent regime episodes over ~30 years, so the effective sample size behind that 'higher Sharpe' is tiny compared to a cross-sectional strategy. An impressive Sharpe on ~5-10 effective observations is not evidence. (2) Daniel/Hodrick/Lu report it as uncorrelated with standard risk factors AND having no downside risk AND a high Sharpe — that combination is what an unexploited-and-unexplained anomaly looks like right before it fails out of sample, and no risk-based story explains it. (3) Verdelhan's 20-90% R-squared range is enormous; the low end of that range is where a retail trader actually lives (short horizons, single pairs). (4) I found NO source showing the dollar carry trade working post-publication (2014 onward). That absence is itself a finding.

**Survives retail costs?** YES on execution costs, and this is the factor most accessible to retail. The dollar factor is implementable with very low turnover in the most liquid instruments on earth (EUR/USD, USD/JPY, GBP/USD, CME FX futures, or a plain dollar-index ETF), so spreads are near-negligible. The catch is that the LRV decomposition attributes $860 of the $1,467 to the interest-rate component — i.e. most of the return IS the carry, which at retail is exactly what the 0.3-0.8% p.a. broker admin fee taxes. Using CME FX futures or the DXY futures complex instead of CFDs avoids the swap markup entirely and is the one genuinely sensible retail route here; no academic source evaluates that implementation.

**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12587>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X1300305X>, <https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/134/1/397/5144784>, <https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/6378/Daniel.Hodrick.Lu.Carry%20Trade.Critical%20Finance%20Review.2017.pdf>

### The FX volatility risk premium is a documented, separate return source — currencies with high implied-minus-realized volatility spreads underperform — but it is effectively INACCESSIBLE to a retail trader.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JFE, JF) — read via search summaries; I could NOT obtain the Sharpe ratio, drawdown, or net-of-cost figures for the Della Corte et al. strategy from any source

**Evidence:** Della Corte, Ramadorai & Sarno (JFE 2016, 120(1):21-40) document that the currency volatility risk premium (expected realized volatility minus model-free implied volatility, i.e. the cost of insuring against currency volatility) predicts the cross-section of currency returns. The strategy sells high-insurance-cost currencies and buys low-insurance-cost ones and has 'highly desirable return and diversification properties.' Crucially, its returns are generated mainly by SPOT exchange rate movements, not interest rate differentials — making it genuinely orthogonal to carry. Separately, Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf (JF 2012) show global FX volatility innovations are the dominant risk factor for carry: high-interest currencies co-move negatively with global volatility while low-interest currencies hedge it, and their volatility proxy accounts for MORE THAN 90% of the return spread across five carry portfolios. Volatility is the deep structure underneath carry.

**Against:** I found no direct refutation, and per the rule that should make me MORE suspicious, not less — this is a comparatively under-replicated corner of the literature and the most likely place for a result that has not yet been stress-tested. Two structural cautions: (1) the strategy requires model-free implied volatility from an FX options surface plus a forecast of realized volatility, both of which are researcher-degrees-of-freedom-rich; (2) it was published in 2016 and I found no post-publication out-of-sample evaluation of it whatsoever.

**Survives retail costs?** NOT APPLICABLE FOR RETAIL — the instrument does not exist for them. FX variance swaps are OTC, dealer-intermediated, carry counterparty default risk, become illiquid precisely in stress, and access is effectively limited to institutions. There is no FX analogue of VIX futures or SVXY. A retail trader attempting to replicate via delta-hedged FX option straddles at retail option spreads would pay a bid-ask on every hedge adjustment and would almost certainly convert the premium into a loss. Building an FX system around this factor is not an option; it is worth knowing about only because it explains WHY carry pays and why carry crashes when volatility spikes.

**Sources:** <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X16300150>, <https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v120y2016i1p21-40.html>, <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2012.01728.x>, <https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/41419708.pdf>

### There is no settled explanation for why carry pays. The risk-based and behavioural camps have each defeated parts of the other, and the honest summary is that the premium is not understood — which is the strongest argument for assuming it can disappear without warning.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JF, JFE, RFS, QJE) — read via search summaries only

**Evidence:** RISK-BASED CAMP: (a) Global FX volatility risk — Menkhoff/Sarno/Schmeling/Schrimpf (JF 2012): high-yield currencies crash when global FX volatility spikes, low-yield currencies hedge it; their volatility factor explains >90% of the carry return spread. (b) Funding liquidity / limits to arbitrage — Brunnermeier/Nagel/Pedersen: the premium is compensation for providing liquidity, and funding-liquidity measures predict exchange rate moves; controlling for liquidity helps explain the UIP puzzle. (c) Downside risk — Lettau, Maggiori & Weber (JFE 2014, 114(2):197-225): a downside-risk CAPM prices the currency cross-section because the market-beta differential between high- and low-yield currencies is larger conditional on BAD market returns, when the price of risk is high; the same model jointly prices equities, commodities, sovereign bonds and currencies. (d) Peso problems — Burnside, Eichenbaum, Kleshchelski & Rebelo (RFS 2011, 24(3):853-891): carry payoffs are large and UNCORRELATED with traditional risk factors, and they attribute this to a peso problem in which the rare event features a very high stochastic discount factor rather than an enormous negative payoff. BEHAVIOURAL/STRUCTURAL CAMP: Menkhoff et al. (JFE 2012) find currency MOMENTUM is not explained by traditional risk factors and is consistent with under- and overreaction, sustained by effective limits to arbitrage. Hassan & Mano (QJE 2019) find the data are not rejected by a model with a permanent cross-sectional asymmetry — some currencies simply pay permanently higher expected returns than others, which is closer to a segmented-market/structural story than to either a risk or a behavioural one.

**Against:** The camps contradict each other, which is the point. Burnside et al. (RFS 2011) find carry payoffs are UNCORRELATED with traditional risk factors — directly opposing the risk-based claim. Lettau et al. and Menkhoff et al. find carry IS priced by downside/volatility risk — directly opposing Burnside. Jurek (JFE 2014) empirically hedges the crash and finds crash-risk premia explain at most one-third of carry returns — undercutting the peso/disaster explanation on its own turf. Daniel, Hodrick & Lu (CFR 2017) find dollar-carry is uncorrelated with standard risk factors and has no downside risk — undercutting risk explanations for the dollar factor entirely. Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen & Vrugt (JFE 2018, 127(2):197-225), covering carry across equities, bonds, commodities, Treasuries, credit and options, conclude carry strategies are 'commonly exposed to global recession, liquidity, and volatility risks, though NONE FULLY EXPLAINS carry's premium.' After ~20 years and dozens of papers, the field's own best summary is that nothing fully explains it.

**Survives retail costs?** Not a cost question, but it has a direct cost implication for the trader: if the premium is compensation for funding-liquidity risk (BNP) or downside risk (Lettau et al.), then a levered retail account IS the marginal constrained speculator being compensated — and is also the first participant forcibly liquidated when the risk realizes. You do not get paid the premium for bearing the risk; you get paid for SURVIVING it, and retail leverage limits (30:1 ESMA, 50:1 US) plus margin-close-out rules mean retail structurally does not survive it.

**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2012.01728.x>, <https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/593088>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X14001378>, <https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/24/3/853/1591669>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X14001081>, <https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:127:y:2018:i:2:p:197-225>, <https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/134/1/397/5144784>

### The FX strategy literature has been explicitly stress-tested for data snooping using reality-check/bootstrap methods, and carry strategies largely FAIL that test out of sample — this is the single most important result for anyone building a rules-based FX system from published findings.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JIMF 2024, JFQA 2009) plus working paper (Hsu/Li/Taylor/Wang post-publication study, SSRN) — read via search summaries; McLean-Pontiff and Harvey-Liu-Zhu figures came via a secondary summary, not the source papers

**Evidence:** Hsu, Taylor & Wang, 'The out-of-sample performance of carry trades' (Journal of International Money and Finance 2024, vol 143) test 1,323 carry trade strategies — rising to 1,339 and 17,199 once learning rules and stop-loss rules are included — across 48 currencies, 1983-2015, applying bootstrap data-snooping corrections. Their conclusion: 'Carry trade strategies chosen as profitable in one period are generally not profitable in an ensuing out-of-sample sample period, ESPECIALLY AFTER CORRECTING FOR DATA-SNOOPING,' and whatever consistency exists 'is concentrated in a relatively brief historical period, 1998-2005.' A companion paper by Hsu, Li, Taylor & Wang examines 13 INFLUENTIAL published carry-trade strategies with bootstrap snooping corrections and finds their profitability DECLINES AFTER THEIR PUBLICATION YEARS. The same authors' earlier work ('Technical Trading: Is it Still Beating the Foreign Exchange Market?') applies the same machinery to FX technical rules. This mirrors Neely, Weller & Ulrich (Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 2009, vol 44), who ran TRUE out-of-sample tests on previously published FX technical rules and found the 1970s-80s excess returns were genuine rather than data-mined, but that filter and moving-average rule profits HAD DISAPPEARED BY THE EARLY 1990s. Broader base rates: McLean & Pontiff find published return predictors decline 26% out-of-sample and 58% post-publication; Harvey, Liu & Zhu argue a newly discovered factor needs a t-statistic above 3.0, not 2.0, given the scale of data mining.

**Against:** Some counterweight exists. Menkhoff et al. (JF 2012) tested 48 currencies over 1983-2009 and the carry spread survived; Accominotti et al. (JFQA 2019) found a 0.55 Sharpe over a century of floating-regime data, which is a genuine long out-of-sample extension of the modern result. Barroso & Santa-Clara (JFQA 2015) ran an explicit 16-year out-of-sample test and their optimal currency portfolio beat naive benchmarks. So 'everything is snooped' is too strong. But note the shape of the disagreement: the surviving results are all DIVERSIFIED, LOW-TURNOVER, MULTI-CURRENCY PORTFOLIO constructions with few parameters. The results that fail out of sample are the ones with entry rules, stop-losses, filters and tuned parameters — precisely the shape of a retail 'trading plan.' Hsu/Taylor/Wang's move from 1,323 to 17,199 strategies once stop-loss rules are added is exactly what happens to the snooping problem when you add the discretionary machinery retail systems are built from.

**Survives retail costs?** This claim is about statistical validity, which is logically prior to costs. But the two compound: a strategy whose true out-of-sample gross edge is near zero after snooping correction has a strictly negative net edge once retail spreads and swap markups are applied. The order of operations matters — apply the snooping haircut first, THEN subtract costs, and most published FX rules are negative before you place a single trade.

### Directly answering the trader's premise: the FX factor literature reports mean returns, standard deviations, Sharpe ratios, skewness and kurtosis — it does not report win rates, because in FX the win rate is nearly uninformative about profitability, and the highest-win-rate strategy in the asset class (carry) is also the one most capable of destroying an account.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Critical Finance Review, NBER Macro Annual) for the specific max gain/loss figures — read via search summary; the general observation that hit rates are absent from this literature is my own survey finding across the papers listed

**Evidence:** Across every paper I surveyed — Brunnermeier/Nagel/Pedersen, Lustig/Roussanov/Verdelhan, Menkhoff et al. (both), Daniel/Hodrick/Lu, Jurek, Koijen et al., Barroso/Santa-Clara, Accominotti et al., Doskov/Swinkels — not one reports a hit rate or win percentage as a headline statistic. The reported risk statistics are the distributional ones, and they exist precisely because the payoff is asymmetric. Daniel, Hodrick & Lu quantify the asymmetry directly: the maximum monthly LOSS is much larger for carry (-6.75% within a month) than for PPP strategies (-4.59%), while the maximum monthly GAIN is smaller for carry (5.09%) than PPP (8.06%). Carry has the worse upside AND the worse downside per month; what it has instead is frequency. Brunnermeier/Nagel/Pedersen's mechanism explains why: losses are amplified by forced unwinding, gains are not amplified. That is a mathematical description of 'wins small and often, loses large and rarely.'

**Against:** Nothing contradicts this — and I want to flag honestly that I could not find any peer-reviewed FX paper that DOES report win rate and payoff ratio together, so I cannot give the expectancy decomposition the brief asked for. That absence is not proof of the claim; it is the limit of what I could verify. If someone produces an FX strategy advertised with a 90% win rate, the numbers that must be demanded are: average win, average loss, worst single loss, worst drawdown, and the number of independent crash episodes in the sample. Carry's sample has roughly four (1998, 2007-09, 2015, 2024) — which is not enough observations to estimate a tail from.

**Survives retail costs?** The asymmetry is made WORSE by costs, not better. Costs are paid on every trade including the winners, so a high-frequency-of-small-wins profile has its edge eroded proportionally more than a low-frequency-of-large-wins profile. A strategy winning 0.3% on 90% of trades and losing 3% on 10% has a gross expectancy of 0.27-0.30 = -0.03% before costs; it is already negative. Add 2 pips round-trip on EUR/USD (~0.018% at 1.10) plus financing and it is decisively negative. High win rate plus retail costs is the worst combination in the asset class.

### Expectancy, not win rate, determines whether a strategy makes money: E = p·W − (1−p)·L, where p is win rate, W average win, L average loss. The break-even payoff ratio is W/L = (1−p)/p. A 90% win rate breaks even at W/L = 0.111, i.e. it survives only while the average loser is under 9x the average winner; a 30% win rate breaks even at W/L = 2.33. Win rate on its own carries zero information about profitability.

**Quality:** model-knowledge-only for the algebra itself (it is textbook probability, not a citable empirical finding). The Kelly/risk-of-ruin restatements I found online were all vendor/blog quality (crosstrade.io, luxalgo.com, journalplus.co) and should NOT be cited as literature. The underlying results trace to Kelly (1956) and Thorp, which I could not fetch.

**Evidence:** Elementary arithmetic, not a research finding. Worked examples: p=0.61 requires W/L ≥ 0.639 to break even; p=0.90 requires W/L ≥ 0.111; p=0.95 requires W/L ≥ 0.0526 (one 19:1 loser cancels all 19 winners). Risk of ruin adds a second, separate constraint: even a positive-expectancy system with too much risk per trade has a material probability of hitting zero first, and the Kelly-optimal fraction f* = (p·b − (1−p))/b produces 40–60% drawdowns at full Kelly, which is why practitioners use 1/4 to 1/2 Kelly.

**Against:** Nothing contradicts the algebra — it is a definition. What IS contestable is the assumption that W and L are stable, independent draws from a fixed distribution. They are not: in FX the loss distribution has a fat left tail (see the carry-crash and SNB claims below), so a backtested expectancy computed from a sample containing no tail event systematically overstates true expectancy. That is the real failure mode, and it is not visible in any win-rate statistic.

**Survives retail costs?** Costs enter directly and hit tight-target strategies hardest. With a 1-pip round-trip cost, a symmetric 10-pip TP / 10-pip SL system has expectancy 20p − 11, so it needs a raw win rate of 55% just to break even. The same system at 100-pip TP / 100-pip SL has expectancy 200p − 101 and breaks even at 50.5%. A 1-pip cost is therefore a 5.0-percentage-point win-rate tax on a 10-pip scalper and a 0.5-point tax on a 100-pip swing trader — a 10x difference. Typical retail EUR/USD all-in cost is roughly 0.7–1.0 pip on a standard spread account, or ~0.0–0.2 pip spread plus $2–$7 per round-turn standard lot (≈0.2–0.7 pip) on an ECN account. This is derived arithmetic plus vendor-quoted cost ranges, not a study.

**Sources:** <https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/risk-of-ruin/>, <https://journalplus.co/metrics/kelly-criterion/>

### In real retail FX account data, the win rate is ABOVE 50% and traders still lose. Ben-David, Birru & Prokopenya report that 62.8% of retail FX trades are winning trades, that losing trades stay open more than twice as long as winners, and that the average loss on a losing trade is more than twice the magnitude of the average win. That combination is mathematically a losing system.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Review of Finance, an A-grade finance journal) for the paper. The specific 62.8% / 2x figures reached me via a search-result summary of the NBER PDF, NOT from my own reading — the proxy blocked nber.org. Verify these two numbers before quoting them to anyone.

**Evidence:** 62.8% win rate with L > 2W gives expectancy ≈ 0.628·W − 0.372·(2W) = −0.116W per trade — negative before any cost. Break-even at p=0.628 requires W/L ≥ 0.592; the observed W/L is below 0.5. Published as Ben-David, Birru & Prokopenya, 'Uninformative Feedback and Risk Taking: Evidence from Retail Forex Trading', Review of Finance 22(6), 2018, 2009–2036; NBER WP 22146. The paper's own thesis is separate and also damaging: traders attribute random wins to skill and increase trade size after winning weeks, even though past performance does not predict future success.

**Against:** Abbey & Doukas (2015, Journal of International Money and Finance 56, 158–177) study 428 individual currency accounts over 2004–2009 and find traders earn positive excess returns even after transaction costs, with day traders outperforming non-day traders on raw, passive-benchmark and risk-adjusted bases. That is a direct, peer-reviewed contradiction of 'retail always loses' and I am reporting it at full strength. Caveats I could verify: n=428 is tiny relative to the hundreds of thousands of accounts in the regulator datasets, it comes from a single platform, the window excludes the post-2015 low-volatility regime, and I found no reality-check/SPA test in the abstract. I searched specifically for published critiques of its selection bias and found none — which means the contradiction stands unrebutted, not that it is wrong.

**Survives retail costs?** The Ben-David et al. result is measured on realised client P&L, so costs are already inside it — the 62.8% win rate is a post-cost, post-spread observation. Adding costs makes it worse, not better. Abbey & Doukas explicitly claim their positive returns survive transaction costs, though I could not read their cost model.

### The Barber-Odean day-trading literature shows that the fraction of speculators with persistent, cost-surviving skill is on the order of 1–5%, and that experience does not fix it — the losers keep trading.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed for the Taiwan papers (Journal of Financial Markets; working papers hosted at Berkeley Haas). The SEC retail-FX quarterly figure reached me only through a search summary and I could NOT locate the underlying SEC document — treat that one as unverified. All PDFs were proxy-blocked.

**Evidence:** Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, 'The Cross-Section of Speculator Skill: Evidence from Day Trading' (Journal of Financial Markets, 2014), covering all Taiwanese day trades 1992–2006: economically large cross-sectional differences in before- and after-fee returns, but few day traders earn positive abnormal returns net of fees; consistently profitable day traders were about 5% of active traders from 1995 to 2006; day traders lost an average of 23.9 basis points per day net of fees. Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, 'Do Individual Day Traders Make Money?': in a typical six-month period more than 80% of day traders lost money after costs, only ~18% were profitable in the following six months, and nearly three-quarters of day-trading volume comes from traders with a history of losses. Their companion learning paper concludes that learning occurs but is slow, suboptimal and costly. SEC reviews of retail FX accounts reportedly found ~70% of customers lost money in each quarter with loss rates persistently high.

**Against:** Two genuine counterweights. (1) The same papers find a real, measurable skill cross-section — a minority DO earn positive abnormal returns net of fees, and past performance predicts future performance for that minority, so 'skill exists' is an empirical finding, not a consolation. (2) The data is Taiwanese equities and futures 1992–2006, not FX, and not retail FX in 2026: the cost structure, leverage limits, product and market microstructure all differ, so transfer to FX is an assumption, not a demonstration. Abbey & Doukas found the opposite sign specifically in FX.

**Survives retail costs?** This is the study that makes costs the whole story: the −23.9 bps/day figure is explicitly NET of fees, and the papers' central framing is the gap between before-fee and after-fee returns. A substantial group of these traders had positive gross returns and negative net returns. Directly applicable to a tight-TP FX plan: the more trades per day, the more times the cost is paid, and the smaller the target, the larger the cost as a share of the target.

### No peer-reviewed study establishes a tradable, cost-surviving win rate for any named chart pattern in FX. The one paper that tested a classic pattern head-on in FX — head-and-shoulders — found it profitable only in a limited sense and DOMINATED by simpler rules.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Economic Journal; Journal of Finance). I could not read either PDF — both hosts were proxy-blocked — so the per-currency breakdown, the cost assumptions and the significance testing in Chang & Osler are unverified by me. If the user wants to pursue chart patterns at all, this is the paper to read in full first.

**Evidence:** Chang & Osler, 'Methodical Madness: Technical Analysis and the Irrationality of Exchange-rate Forecasts', Economic Journal 109(458), 1999, 636–661: evaluates head-and-shoulders forecasts on daily dollar exchange rates 1973–1994 against two rationality criteria, profitability and efficiency. Finding: the rule is profitable but NOT efficient, because it is dominated by simpler trading rules. **Against:** Chang & Osler DID find head-and-shoulders profitable, which cuts against a blanket 'chart patterns are worthless'. Lo et al. DID find statistically detectable incremental information in patterns. I am reporting both at full strength. ### The FX technical-rule literature is heavily contaminated by data snooping, and the rule profits that survived reality-check testing had largely disappeared by the early 1990s — before any of today's retail patterns were marketed.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JFQA; Journal of Economic Surveys; Qi & Wu published in Journal of Money, Credit and Banking). This is the strongest methodological evidence in the whole file. All PDFs proxy-blocked; figures from search summaries.

**Evidence:** Neely, Weller & Ulrich, 'The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis: Evidence from the Foreign Exchange Market', Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 44, 2009: conducts TRUE out-of-sample tests on previously published rules and concludes the excess returns of the 1970s and 1980s were genuine and not data-mined, BUT the profit opportunities had disappeared by the early 1990s for filter and moving-average rules; returns to less-studied rules also declined. Qi & Wu (2006): applies White's (2000) Reality Check to 2,127 technical trading rules on seven daily dollar exchange rates 1973–1998 — the first FX application of the Reality Check; data-snooping bias does not overturn full-sample conclusions, but sub-sample analysis shows the snooping problem is more serious in the second half and profitability is much weaker in the recent period. Park & Irwin, 'What Do We Know About the Profitability of Technical Analysis?', Journal of Economic Surveys 21(4), 2007: of 95 modern studies, 56 positive, 20 negative, 19 mixed — but the authors' own conclusion is that most empirical studies suffer from data snooping, ex post selection of rules or search technologies, and difficulties estimating risk and transaction costs.

**Against:** The 56-of-95 positive count is a real counterweight and I will not bury it: a majority of studies found technical rules profitable. Park & Irwin themselves say modern studies indicate technical strategies consistently generated economic profits in a variety of speculative markets at least until the early 1990s. Qi & Wu found the full-sample conclusions survived the Reality Check. The honest reading is not 'technical analysis never worked' but 'it worked, it was arbitraged away in FX by roughly the early 1990s, and the published positive results are inflated by the studies that never corrected for search'. That 56/20/19 vote count is itself a publication-bias artifact — nobody publishes the rule that did not work.

**Survives retail costs?** Park & Irwin name transaction-cost estimation as one of the three systematic flaws in the literature, which means many of the 56 positive studies likely overstate net returns. Neely-Weller-Ulrich's disappearance result is a gross-return finding; costs make the post-1990 picture worse. Practical implication for the user: any backtested pattern win rate produced today, on a market that has been mined by every retail platform for 30 years, should be assumed to be a search artifact until it passes an SPA/Reality Check test and a genuine out-of-sample split with realistic spreads.

### The canonical high-win-rate FX strategy — the carry trade — is peer-reviewed evidence that a long string of small wins is bought with negatively skewed crash risk, not with edge.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed / top-tier working paper (NBER Macroeconomics Annual). I could not fetch the PDF — nber.org and fmg.ac.uk were both proxy-blocked — so I do not have the exact skewness coefficients or Sharpe ratios. Those specific numbers should be pulled from the paper before being used.

**Evidence:** Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen, 'Carry Trades and Currency Crashes', NBER Macroeconomics Annual 2008 (vol 23, 313–347), NBER WP 14473: exchange-rate movements between high- and low-interest-rate currencies exhibit negative skewness; the negative skewness comes from sudden unwinding of carry trades, which happens when risk appetite and funding liquidity fall. Funding-liquidity measures predict exchange-rate movements. The structure is exactly the pattern the user should fear: many small positive carry accruals, punctuated by rare large adverse moves — 'up by the stairs, down by the elevator' (I could NOT verify that specific phrase appears in this paper; treat it as folklore, not a quotation).

**Against:** The carry trade has genuinely earned positive average returns over long samples — that is why it is one of the most studied strategies in international finance, and the paper is explaining a real profit, not debunking a fake one. Some of the literature argues the crash risk is a compensated risk premium rather than a flaw, which would make carry a legitimate (if lumpy) edge rather than a trap. The point for a retail trader is narrower and survives that debate: the win rate of carry is very high and tells you nothing about the risk you are being paid to hold.

**Survives retail costs?** Carry is the one FX strategy where the swap/rollover line is the SOURCE of return rather than a cost — but retail swap credits are marked down from interbank rates by the broker, so the retail version of the carry trade earns materially less than the academic version while bearing the identical crash risk. I could not find a study quantifying the retail swap haircut; that gap matters and should be measured directly from the user's own broker's swap table before any carry-flavoured plan is built.

### In FX specifically, a stop-loss does not bound your loss. The 15 January 2015 SNB de-peg is the documented proof: clients ended up owing money beyond their account equity, and institutions died.

**Quality:** financial press and company statements (CNN, Bloomberg, CNBC, Finance Magnates, swissinfo, GlobeNewswire) plus an SEC administrative proceeding against Everest Capital and its principal for misconduct relating to risk management. Not academic, but these are contemporaneous reported facts about named institutions, and the SEC order is an official document. High confidence in the events; the dollar figures are as reported by the firms themselves.

**Evidence:** FXCM announced clients generated negative equity balances owed to FXCM of approximately $225 million after EUR/CHF volatility on 15 Jan 2015, putting it in possible breach of regulatory capital requirements and forcing ~$300m of emergency financing; FXCM stated its liquidity providers ceased providing pricing during the event, which is why stops did not fill. Alpari (UK) entered insolvency on 16 Jan 2015, stating the majority of clients sustained losses exceeding their account equity and that where a client cannot cover the loss it passes to Alpari. Everest Capital's $830m Global fund — its oldest and largest — was wiped out; the franc rose more than 30% versus the euro. Saxo Bank repriced trades and pursued clients for roughly $100m, and reported a DKK 645m (~$98m) net loss for 2015.

**Against:** Post-2015 regulation has materially changed this for EU/UK retail clients: ESMA's product intervention mandates negative balance protection on a per-account basis and a margin close-out rule, so an EU/UK retail account today should not be able to go below zero. That is a real and important mitigation and I am not going to understate it. What negative balance protection does NOT do is prevent the account going TO zero in one gap — it caps the loss at 100% of equity, which is still a total loss. And it does not apply to offshore brokers, which is where many retail traders chasing high leverage end up.

**Survives retail costs?** This is the cost that no backtest contains. A gap-through stop is a slippage event of unbounded size: on 15 Jan 2015 EUR/CHF moved more than 30% with no quotes in between, so a '20-pip stop' filled thousands of pips away. Any expectancy calculation built on the assumption that the average loss equals the stop distance is therefore wrong in exactly the direction that matters, and a high-win-rate plan is wrong by the most, because its entire equity curve is made of small wins it cannot use to absorb one such loss.

### No position-sizing scheme — martingale, grid, averaging down, 'recovery' — can convert a negative-expectancy edge into a positive one. This is a theorem, not an opinion, and it is the reason 95%-win-rate robots exist and then do not.

**Quality:** model-knowledge-only / textbook probability. The sources I could find stating it are Wikipedia, a UNLV gaming-institute paper on the roulette martingale, lecture notes, and MQL5 trading blogs — I found NO peer-reviewed study of martingale/grid EA failure rates in FX, and the popular write-ups ('The Mathematics of Ruin: Why Your 95% Win Rate Robot is a Ticking Financial Bomb') are blog quality and should not be cited as evidence. The mathematics is nonetheless not in dispute.

**Evidence:** The expected value of a series of bets is the sum over all bets of (expected value of that bet × probability the player makes it); if each individual bet has negative expectation, the sum of negative terms is negative regardless of the staking rule. The impossibility of winning over the long run given any limit on bet size, bankroll or credit is proven by the optional stopping theorem; martingale fails even with unbounded stopping time as long as there is a limit on earnings or bet size. Only with simultaneously unbounded wealth, unbounded bet size and unbounded time does martingale become a 'winning' strategy — and a retail FX account has margin limits, so it has none of the three.

**Against:** I found no credible source arguing martingale works, and per rule 3 I treat that one-sidedness with suspicion — but here the asymmetry is explained by the fact that the claim is a proof rather than an empirical regularity, so there is nothing to find on the other side. The honest qualifier is different: martingale/grid systems are not merely negative-expectancy, they are expectancy-NEUTRAL transformations. They take whatever edge you had and redistribute the outcome distribution into many small wins plus a rare total loss. If the underlying edge were genuinely positive, a grid would still not destroy it — it would just make the drawdown path far worse and the risk of ruin far higher. The killer is the combination of no edge AND a ruin-maximising sizing rule.

**Survives retail costs?** No, and costs make it strictly worse in a way that is easy to miss: a grid opens many positions, so it pays the spread many times per 'sequence', and it holds losers for a long time by design, so it pays swap on the losing side repeatedly. A martingale sequence that recovers to break-even on price has still lost money on accumulated spread and negative carry. I could not find a study quantifying this; it is arithmetic.

**Sources:** <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martingale_(betting_system)>, <https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1630&context=gaming_institute>, <https://ionides.github.io/620/notes/martingales.pdf>

### The behavioural mechanism that manufactures a high win rate in retail FX is the disposition effect: traders close winners fast and hold losers, which mechanically raises the win rate while lowering expectancy. Retail FX data shows this directly, and shows that stop/limit orders reverse it.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (European Journal of Finance; Review of Financial Studies). PDFs proxy-blocked; details from search summaries. The '50% higher rate' figure came through a search summary without a clear attribution and I would not quote it.

**Evidence:** Nolte, 'A detailed investigation of the disposition effect and individual trading behavior: a panel survival approach', European Journal of Finance 18(10), 2012: uses FX trader data; finds an INVERTED disposition effect for small profits and losses and the usual positive disposition effect for larger ones, and finds the inverted effect is driven to a great extent by patient and cautious investors closing positions with take-profit and stop-loss orders. Heimer, 'Peer Pressure: Social Interaction and the Disposition Effect', Review of Financial Studies 29(11), 2016, 3177–3209: uses an investment-specific social network linked to individual FX trading records; access to the social network nearly DOUBLES the magnitude of a trader's disposition effect, and connected traders develop correlated levels of it. Ben-David/Birru/Prokopenya (above): losing trades open more than twice as long as winners. A separate analysis reports retail traders realise winners at roughly a 50% higher rate than losers.

**Against:** Nolte's result is genuinely two-sided and is the most useful finding here for someone building a plan: mechanical stop-loss and take-profit orders INVERT the disposition effect, i.e. pre-committing exits fixes the behavioural leak. That is evidence FOR having a written plan with fixed exits, which is what the user is trying to build. It does not, however, say anything about which entry pattern to use, and it does not create an edge — it removes a specific self-inflicted loss.

**Survives retail costs?** The disposition effect is a P&L-destroying behaviour independent of costs, so removing it is a free improvement. But note the trap it sets: the 'fix' most retail traders reach for is a tight take-profit and a wide stop, which mechanically produces the high win rate they are shopping for and reproduces exactly the 48-pip-winner / 83-pip-loser distribution FXCM measured. The Heimer result adds a second warning directly relevant to this user's request: copying what a social network or signal group is doing roughly doubles the bias.

**Sources:** <https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/29/11/3177/2583763>, <https://www.clevelandfed.org/-/media/project/clevelandfedtenant/clevelandfedsite/publications/working-papers/2016/wp-1618-peer-pressure-social-interaction-and-the-disposition-effect.pdf>, <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1351847x.2011.601635>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=911528>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S154461232501517X>

### Retail FX trading as a whole does not outperform random trading — a finding that directly undercuts the premise that some pattern-based plan is being systematically discovered and exploited by retail.

**Quality:** working paper / institutional repository, not peer-reviewed journal publication as far as I could establish. Lower tier than the Review of Finance and JFQA material above, but it is academic in form and directly on FX. ## FX market microstructure and order flow: what actually predicts exchange rates at short horizons, whether intraday/session/fix effects are tradeable net of retail costs, and what data a retail account can actually obtain.

METHOD CAVEAT THE PARENT AGENT MUST PROPAGATE: WebFetch was blocked at the network egress proxy for every domain I tried (nber.org, bis.org, sciencedirect, ssrn, arxiv, snb.ch, repec, wikipedia, university hosts). I could run WebSearch but could NOT open a single full paper. Every claim below therefore rests on (a) search-engine summaries of real, correctly-identified, citable papers, plus (b) my own model knowledge. I have labelled which is which. Numbers quoted from search summaries are second-hand and have NOT been checked against the papers' tables. Anyone building on this should re-verify the numeric claims (especially the Sharpe ratios and the effective-spread figures) against the actual PDFs before risking money.

HEADLINE FOR THE TRADER: order flow is the best-documented short-horizon predictor in FX — and it is the one input a retail account structurally cannot obtain. The version of order flow that is sold to retail is specifically the version that has been tested and found not to forecast (Sager & Taylor 2008). The session and fix effects are real and survive *interbank* costs in a narrow set of cases, and the literature reports Sharpe ratios, never win rates — the "high win rate pattern" framing has no counterpart anywhere in this body of work.

### The famous Evans & Lyons result — order flow 'explains' 40-60%+ of daily exchange rate variation — is a CONTEMPORANEOUS regression, not a forecast. Same-day order flow is regressed on same-day returns. It is not a trading signal, because to use it you would need to know today's net order flow before today's price moved.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JPE) — but abstract/search-summary verified only; full text NOT fetched (egress blocked). The contemporaneous-vs-predictive structure is also model-knowledge, and it is the single most important thing a retail trader must understand about this paper.

**Evidence:** Evans & Lyons (2002, Journal of Political Economy 110(1):170-180). Daily DM/USD and JPY/USD, 4 months of interdealer data from Reuters D2000-1, May-Aug 1996. Search summaries report R² 'above 50 percent' and elsewhere 'approximately 60 percent' of $/DM daily variation, against R² of ~10% being rare for macro exchange-rate models. The regression is Δp_t = f(order flow_t) — contemporaneous. The authors themselves decompose flow into expected and unexpected components and find the explanatory power comes from the UNEXPECTED component, which is by construction not forecastable.

**Against:** Nothing contradicts the R² itself; it replicates widely. What contradicts the TRADING interpretation: the regression has an endogeneity/simultaneity problem acknowledged in the paper — price moves and order flow are jointly determined, and a dealer quoting a new price induces flow as much as flow induces price. A high contemporaneous R² between two simultaneously determined variables is close to an accounting identity and carries no forecasting content. Sager & Taylor (2008) and Berger et al. (2005) both find the relationship weakens sharply or vanishes once you lag the flow.

**Survives retail costs?** Not applicable — there is no implementable trade here, so costs never enter. This is the point: a 60% R² that cannot be traded has an expectancy of exactly zero minus whatever you spend trying. Any vendor or educator citing 'order flow explains 60% of FX moves' as a reason to buy an order-flow product is misrepresenting a contemporaneous regression as a predictive one.

**Sources:** <https://www.nber.org/papers/w7317>, <https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v110y2002i1p170-180.html>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=174888>, <https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=3821510>

### The price-impact magnitude in Evans & Lyons is roughly 0.5% (≈50bp) of DM/USD per USD 1 billion of net dollar purchases. This quantifies why a retail trader is invisible to the market and cannot be on the causal side of this relationship.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JPE) — search-summary verified, table not checked. Treat the 0.5%/$1bn figure as approximately right and era-specific (1996 data, pre-electronification).

**Evidence:** Evans & Lyons (2002), search summary: 'For the DM/$ spot market as a whole, $1 billion of net dollar purchases increases the DM price of a dollar by about 0.5 percent.'

**Against:** The coefficient is not stable across eras or venues. Berger et al. (2005, Fed IFDP 830) using 1999-2004 EBS data at 1-minute frequency find the flow/return association is substantially weaker at longer horizons, and the electronification and internalisation of FX since 2004 (dealers now net client flow internally rather than pushing it to the interdealer market) means far less of total flow reaches an observable venue at all. So the modern coefficient on OBSERVABLE flow is very likely smaller and noisier than 1996's.

**Survives retail costs?** Irrelevant to a retail account by construction. A 1-standard-lot trade is $100k = 0.01% of the $1bn unit — implied price impact ~0.005bp, i.e. zero. Retail is a price taker paying the spread to someone who is not. Scaling this result down to a retail position size is a category error, not a strategy.

**Sources:** <https://www.nber.org/papers/w7317>, <https://cepii.fr/IE/rev96/rev96lyons.pdf>, <https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/ifdp/2005/830/ifdp830.pdf>

### The genuinely PREDICTIVE (lagged) window for interdealer order flow is on the order of 1 to 5 minutes, and the predictability is gone by 30 minutes. This is the actual short-horizon edge the microstructure literature documents — and it sits in exactly the horizon band where retail transaction costs are largest relative to the move.

**Quality:** working-paper (NBER; subsequently published in an NBER conference volume) — search-summary verified, full text not fetched.

**Evidence:** Ito & Hashimoto, 'Price Impacts of Deals and Predictability of the Exchange Rate Movements' (NBER WP 12682, 2006; later in an NBER volume, 2008), using proprietary EBS tick data. Search summary: 'price impacts of deals at one side of the market are significant but short-lived'; forecasting equations for the next X minutes (X = 1, 5, 15, 30) give coefficients significantly different from zero at 1-min and 5-min horizons, 'but the significance disappears in the 30-minute interval,' with t-statistics rising as the window shortens.

**Against:** Evans & Lyons (2005 AER 'Meese-Rogoff Redux') claim micro-based forecasts beat a random walk at horizons from one day to one month, with micro-based forecasts accounting for ~16% of sample variance in MONTHLY spot changes — a much longer horizon than Ito & Hashimoto's minutes. Those two findings are hard to reconcile, and the longer-horizon claim rests on proprietary CUSTOMER flow (a different, richer variable) rather than interdealer flow. Sager & Taylor (2008) failed to reproduce robust forecasting power with the data an outsider can buy. Berger et al. (2005) independently report the association is 'substantially weaker at longer horizons.' The weight of evidence is: real but short-lived.

**Survives retail costs?** Almost certainly not for retail. A 1-5 minute EUR/USD move worth capturing is typically a small number of pips. Best-case retail round-trip cost on EUR/USD is ~0.6bp (see cost claim below); a strategy needing many round trips per day to harvest minute-scale predictability pays that on every one. Ito & Hashimoto's own framing is conditional on a participant who 'can observe and analyze ALL the transactions information in real time' — that is an EBS/interdealer seat, not a retail terminal. No retail account sees consolidated FX tape in real time, because no consolidated FX tape exists.

### DISAGGREGATED CUSTOMER order flow — broken out by counterparty type (asset managers, hedge funds, corporates, private clients) — genuinely does predict future exchange rates with large economic value. This is the strongest positive result in the whole angle. It is also built entirely on proprietary bank data that a retail trader cannot buy at any price.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed, top-tier (Journal of Finance; Journal of Financial Economics) — search-summary verified, full texts NOT fetched. The Sharpe figures of 1.26/1.45 are second-hand from a search summary and must be checked in the paper.

**Evidence:** Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf, 'Information Flows in Foreign Exchange Markets: Dissecting Customer Currency Trades', BIS WP 405 (2013), published Journal of Finance 71(2):601-633 (2016). Proprietary disaggregated customer flow from a major global dealer across a broad cross-section of currencies. Search summary reports portfolios sorted on buying pressure produce a high-minus-low spread of 10.31% and 12.43% per annum with Sharpe ratios of 1.26 and 1.45, described as a 'dollar-neutral out-of-sample trading strategy that only conditions on real-time information.' Customer groups 'differ markedly in their predictive ability, trading styles, and risk exposure'; corporates are described as buying in falling markets and selling in rising ones (i.e. liquidity providers, not informed traders). Corroborated in spirit by Ranaldo & Somogyi, 'Asymmetric Information Risk in FX Markets', Journal of Financial Economics 140(2):391-411 (2021), which finds 'heterogeneous superior information across agents, time and currency pairs' and builds a long-short strategy on the informed component of flow.

**Against:** Three serious caveats, two of which are in the literature itself. (1) Sager & Taylor (2008) tested COMMERCIALLY AVAILABLE order flow — the kind an outsider can actually buy — and found no robust forecasting power. So the result is specific to the proprietary, counterparty-labelled version. (2) Evans & Lyons' own longer-horizon claims were never cleanly replicated by outsiders precisely because the data is not shareable, which means this entire sub-literature is effectively un-replicable by third parties — a structural, not incidental, weakness. (3) One dealer's customer book is a non-random sample of global flow; the result may partly reflect that dealer's client mix.

**Survives retail costs?** UNRESOLVED, and this is a red flag I could not clear. I could not verify from any source I reached whether the 10-12% p.a. / Sharpe 1.26-1.45 figures are gross or net of bid-ask spreads. A separate FX study surfaced in the same search reported 4.05% return / Sharpe 0.83 BEFORE costs falling to 3.16% / Sharpe 0.65 AFTER costs using quoted bid-ask — i.e. roughly a 20-25% haircut to Sharpe at INSTITUTIONAL spreads. At retail spreads the haircut is far larger. But this is all moot for the user: the input data is unobtainable, so the cost question never arises. Do not let a Sharpe of 1.45 from an unobtainable dataset anchor expectations for a strategy built on obtainable data.

**Sources:** <https://www.bis.org/publ/work405.pdf>, <https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-405-information-flows-foreign-exchange-markets-dissecting-customer-currency-trades.pdf>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2062257>, <https://ideas.repec.org/p/bis/biswps/405.html>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3263279>, <https://ux-tauri.unisg.ch/RePEc/usg/sfwpfi/WPF-1820.pdf>

### THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT PAPER FOR THIS USER: order flow data that is commercially available to outsiders does NOT robustly forecast exchange rates. The gap between the academic result and the purchasable product is the whole game.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JMCB, a strong journal) — search-summary verified, full text not fetched. Note this is a 55-page paper, i.e. a serious treatment, not a note.

**Evidence:** Sager & Taylor, 'Commercially Available Order Flow Data and Exchange Rate Movements: Caveat Emptor', Journal of Money, Credit and Banking 40(4):583-625 (June 2008). They test both interdealer and commercially available order flow data. Search summaries: they 'fail to find robust predictive power of exchange rates by order flow in the first place, using commercially available order flow data'; 'no clear cut evidence that order flow models of the exchange rate perform better than a simple random walk model in out-of-sample forecasting'; and the contemporaneous result is 'weaker when a lagged order flow model is used.' The title — 'let the buyer beware' — is the authors' own framing and is aimed squarely at people buying order flow products.

**Against:** Evans & Lyons (2002, 2005) and Menkhoff et al. **Survives retail costs?** The finding is that there is nothing to survive — no reliable gross signal in purchasable data, so costs are academic. This directly falsifies the retail 'order flow trading' product category as it is usually sold. Any vendor selling 'institutional order flow' to retail should be asked to name their data source and show a cost-adjusted out-of-sample test; Sager & Taylor is the prior they have to beat.

### WHY order flow predicts when charts do not: order flow is a direct measure of aggregated private information and dealer inventory pressure; a chart is a lagged, public, universally-observed function of past prices only. The asymmetry is informational, not technical — and it is exactly the asymmetry that excludes retail.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed for the underlying papers; the synthesis sentence is my own and is model-knowledge-only. The mechanism is the standard account in the field, not a fringe view.

**Evidence:** This is the central theoretical claim of the Evans-Lyons microstructure programme (Evans & Lyons 2002 JPE; Evans & Lyons 'Understanding Order Flow', International Journal of Finance & Economics 11(1):3-23, 2006). The mechanism: heterogeneous agents hold dispersed private information about fundamentals and their own hedging/liquidity needs; that information reaches the price only when someone trades on it; dealers observe the resulting flow and update quotes. Rime, Sarno & Sojli (2010) formalise the version where order flow reflects heterogeneous expectations about macro fundamentals and the market learns the state of the economy gradually. Menkhoff et al. (2016) and Ranaldo & Somogyi (2021) supply the cross-sectional evidence that some counterparty types are informed and others (corporates) are not.

**Against:** The mechanism cuts against the user's premise in a way worth stating plainly. If prices move because private information arrives via flow, then a chart pattern — which contains only public past prices — is the LEAST informative object in the room, and its edge should be competed away fastest because everyone can see it. Hsu & Kuan and Qi & Wu (see data-snooping claim) find exactly that empirically: FX technical-rule profitability weakens materially in later subsamples. Contradicting evidence in the other direction: Neely, Weller & Dittmar (1997) found genetically-programmed technical rules profitable out-of-sample for 1981-1995 — but that is a pre-electronification sample and the same authors later documented the decay.

**Survives retail costs?** Not a tradeable claim in itself. Its practical consequence is a cost claim though: if the informative variable is unobservable to you and the observable variable (price history) is public and competed, then your realistic expected gross edge from chart patterns is near zero, while your costs are certain and measurable. Near-zero gross minus certain costs is a negative-expectancy business regardless of win rate.

**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ijfe.287>, <https://www.nber.org/papers/w7317>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022199609000464>, <https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2011/2011-001.pdf>

### INTRADAY SEASONALITY IS REAL: currencies show a statistically significant tendency to DEPRECIATE during their own local trading hours, driven by local participants being net buyers of foreign currency in their own session. But net of even institutional bid-ask spreads, only EUR/USD was profitable — one pair out of six.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JMCB) + central-bank working paper — search-summary verified, full text not fetched. I could NOT obtain the effect magnitude in basis points, nor the annualised net return, nor the reported Sharpe. Those are the three numbers that would decide tradeability and I do not have them.

**Evidence:** Breedon & Ranaldo, 'Intraday Patterns in FX Returns and Order Flow', SNB Working Paper 2011-4, published Journal of Money, Credit and Banking 45(5):953-965 (2013). Hourly spot data, six major pairs, Jan 1997-May 2007. Search summaries: 'a significant tendency for currencies to depreciate during local trading hours,' confirmed 'across a range of currencies and time zones'; the pattern shows up in order flow and reflects 'the tendency of market participants to be net purchasers of foreign exchange in their own trading hours.' Critically, one summary states: 'only EUR-USD showing net profitability after bid-ask spreads.'

**Against:** Four things, and they are serious. (1) The sample ENDS MAY 2007 — nineteen years stale as of today, and it predates the post-crisis collapse in rate differentials, the internalisation of client flow, and the algo-execution era. I found NO verifiable post-2015 replication. (2) 'Only EUR/USD net profitable after spreads' means five of six pairs FAILED the cost test at institutional spreads, which is itself a strong hint the raw effect is small relative to frictions. (3) The pattern is a well-known published anomaly; publication is itself a decay mechanism. (4) I could not determine whether the paper applied any reality-check / SPA / multiple-testing correction, and with six pairs × 24 hourly buckets the multiple-comparisons surface is non-trivial.

**Survives retail costs?** For interbank: marginally, for EUR/USD only. For RETAIL: probably not, by my own arithmetic (label this as my calculation, not the paper's). The strategy requires one round trip per day. Best-case retail EUR/USD cost is ~0.1 pip spread + ~$5.50/lot commission ≈ 0.64bp round trip; 250 trading days × 0.64bp ≈ 160bp ≈ 1.6% per year in costs alone. On a typical commission-free retail account at ~1.2 pips ≈ 1.11bp round trip, that is ~278bp ≈ 2.8% per year. A raw effect that only just cleared institutional spreads on one pair in a sample ending in 2007 has essentially no room left after a 1.6-2.8% annual retail cost drag — before slippage and before any swap/rollover charge on positions held across 5pm NY.

### Benchmark FIXINGS create a documented, pervasive 'W-shaped' intraday return pattern: the USD appreciates into the fixes and depreciates afterwards, around the Tokyo, Frankfurt and London fixings. The authors state these patterns are exploitable after transaction costs — but by arbitrageurs, at arbitrageur cost levels.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed, top-tier (Journal of Finance, 2024) — search-summary verified, full text not fetched. Strong design: 21 years, nine currencies, natural experiments for identification, a stated economic mechanism (dealer inventory), and an explicit cost adjustment. This is the best-evidenced tradeable-looking effect in the whole angle.

**Evidence:** Krohn, Mueller & Whelan, 'Foreign Exchange Fixings and Returns Around the Clock', Journal of Finance 79(1):541-578 (Feb 2024). Top nine traded currencies, 21-year sample. Search summary: 'The U.S. dollar appreciates in the run-up to foreign exchange (FX) fixes and depreciates thereafter, tracing a W-shaped return pattern around the clock'; reversals are 'pervasive and highly statistically significant' and imply 'daily swings of more than one billion U.S. dollars based on spot volumes'; the mechanism is inventory risk from dealers intermediating unconditional USD demand at the fixes; the effect is 'unique to the U.S. dollar numeraire, amplified in states of high anticipated volatility, low liquidity,' and 'arbitrageurs can exploit these patterns after taking transaction costs into account.' Natural-experiment identification: the existence of a published reference rate determines the timing of the reversals.

**Against:** The cost statement is the crux and I could not verify what cost level 'arbitrageurs' means in their tables — institutional effective spreads are roughly 0.31bp on EUR/USD (Mancini et al. 2013) versus ~0.6-1.1bp+ for the best retail accounts, and spreads WIDEN inside the fixing window when the effect is concentrated. Also: the effect is a reversal around a moment of extreme volatility, so realised slippage on a retail market order will materially exceed the quoted spread. Michelberger & Witte (Journal of Finance and Data Science, 2016) independently document 'spiking volatility within the WM/R 4pm fixing window' and 'an increased probability for spot rate extrema' during it. And Ito & Yamada (NBER WP 23327) find the 2015 reform changed bank behaviour — the volume spike vanished — so post-2015 dynamics differ from the historical sample.

**Survives retail costs?** At institutional cost levels: the authors say yes. At retail cost levels: NOT ESTABLISHED, and I would bet against it. This is a daily round-trip strategy, so it carries the same ~1.6-2.8%/yr retail cost drag computed above (my arithmetic), but executed at the single worst moment of the day for spread and slippage. A retail trader would be taking the other side of, specifically, the best-resourced execution desks in the world at the one minute they care most about. The honest verdict is: real effect, wrong participant.

**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jofi.13306>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3521370>, <https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/177333/1/WRAP-foreign-exchange-fixings-returns-around-clock-Mueller-2023.pdf>, <https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v79y2024i1p541-578.html>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405918816300010>, <https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1501.07778>

### The 4pm London (WM/Reuters) fix specifically shows extraordinary volatility and strong NEGATIVE serial correlation — price spikes into the fix and reverses out of it — and the effect is strongest on the last trading day of the month, driven by predictable equity-hedging rebalancing flow.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JBF; JIFMIM) — search-summary verified, full texts not fetched. Melvin & Prins also circulated via an ECB FX workshop, so the working paper is central-bank-vetted.

**Evidence:** Three converging papers. (1) Evans, 'Forex trading and the WMR Fix', Journal of Banking & Finance 87:233-247 (2018): 21 currencies over a decade; 'forex price changes display extraordinary volatility and negative serial correlation around the Fix,' and the paper notes this is CONTRARY to the predictions of the microstructure model — i.e. an unexplained anomaly, not a confirmation. (2) Melvin & Prins, 'Equity hedging and exchange rates at the London 4 p.m. fix', Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money (2015): 'Equity market appreciation over the month predicts currency depreciation before the end-of-month fix'; hedging demand has 'a strong, but temporary, impact on exchange rates'; 'pre- and post-Fix changes in spot rates are extraordinarily volatile and exhibit strong negative serial correlation, particularly on the last trading day of each month.' (3) Their striking observation: the hedgers' behaviour is 'predictable in advance and at least in theory, known to the rest of the market' and STILL moves price — i.e. the anomaly persists despite being public.

**Against:** Ito & Yamada, 'Did the Reform Fix the London Fix Problem?' (NBER WP 23327, 2017) is the key complication: after the Feb 2015 reform the volume spike in the fixing window DISAPPEARED, but 'the anomalies on price dynamics reported in the previous literature still exist, and banks' passive trading strategy generates ANOTHER predictability in the price movement.' So the specific historical pattern was altered by the reform — meaning a backtest on pre-2015 fix data does not describe the post-2015 market. Also, all three papers document volatility and reversal; none that I could verify reports a net-of-cost retail-scale strategy return, a hit rate, or a payoff ratio.

**Survives retail costs?** NOT DEMONSTRATED for retail at any point in this literature. Month-end-only trading reduces the round-trip count dramatically (12/yr vs 250/yr), which is the one structural point in retail's favour here — 12 × 1.11bp ≈ 13bp/yr of cost is trivially affordable. But that advantage is offset by: the widest spreads of the month, the highest slippage, a signal (month-end equity return) that is public to every desk, and an effect that Ito & Yamada show has already shifted once in response to regulation. I could not find any study quantifying the net retail-executable return, and I am not willing to infer one.

### NOT A SINGLE PAPER IN THIS ENTIRE LITERATURE REPORTS A WIN RATE. The microstructure literature measures edge in Sharpe ratios, annualised excess returns, R², and t-statistics. The concept the user is organising their system around does not appear in the serious research at all.

That would not change the conclusion, because a statistic that never makes an abstract is a statistic the field does not consider decision-relevant. The reason is structural: Sharpe and expectancy are invariant to how you chop a return stream into 'trades,' whereas win rate is entirely an artefact of where you place your stop and target. You can manufacture any win rate you like between 1% and 99% on the SAME underlying return stream purely by moving the exits. Win rate is therefore not a property of an edge; it is a property of your exit policy.

**Survives retail costs?** Directly relevant. Because win rate is an exit-policy artefact, the standard way to manufacture a high win rate is a tight target and a wide (or absent) stop — which maximises the number of round trips per unit of profit captured and therefore maximises the cost drag, while concentrating all the risk in the rare large loss. A 90%-win FX system with a 1:9 payoff has zero gross expectancy BEFORE costs and is a guaranteed loser after them. The 'highest win rate' objective, applied in FX, actively selects for the worst possible cost-to-edge ratio.

### DATA SNOOPING: when FX technical-rule profitability is tested with White's Reality Check and Hansen's SPA test, the apparent edge weakens sharply in later subsamples — the data-snooping problem is explicitly found to be MORE serious in the second half of samples. The FX technical literature is exactly as compromised as the user's rule 6 assumes.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed and official (St. Louis Fed working paper; Hsu & Kuan; Qi & Wu) — search-summary verified, full texts not fetched. The St. Louis Fed survey is the highest-quality single entry point here and is freely available.

**Evidence:** Qi & Wu applied White's Reality Check to FX and found profitability survives over 1973-1998 but is 'considerably weaker for their 1986 to 1998 subsample.' A Reality-Check FX study summarised in search finds 'data-snooping biases do not change basic conclusions for the full sample, but a sub-sample analysis indicates the data-snooping problem is more serious in the second half of the sample, with profitability becoming much weaker in the more recent period, suggesting the foreign exchange market becomes more efficient over time.' Hsu & Kuan, 'Re-Examining the Profitability of Technical Analysis with White's Reality Check and Hansen's SPA Test' — the methodological reference for quantifying snooping by testing the best rule against the full universe of rules searched. Neely & Weller's survey, 'Technical Analysis in the Foreign Exchange Market' (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis WP 2011-001), is the standard review and documents the decline. Neely, Weller & Dittmar (1997) found genetic-programming rules profitable out-of-sample for 1981-1995 — the positive result, from a pre-electronification era.

**Against:** The pre-1990s positive results are real and were obtained out-of-sample (Neely, Weller & Dittmar used genuine OOS periods). So 'technical rules never worked in FX' is too strong. The defensible statement is: they worked in the 1970s-80s, decayed through the 1990s, and the decay coincides with electronification, falling transaction costs for institutions, and the publication of the results themselves. The adaptive-markets reading — edges exist, get discovered, get arbitraged — fits the evidence better than either extreme.

**Survives retail costs?** This is the crux for the user's actual question. Most FX technical-rule studies test on interbank or indicative quotes with institutional or zero cost assumptions. The profitability that 'becomes much weaker' in recent subsamples is measured GROSS or at institutional cost. Layering a retail cost base (~0.6-1.1bp per round trip on the best pair, more elsewhere, plus swap and slippage) onto an already-decayed gross edge is what turns a marginal academic result into a reliably negative-expectancy retail system. Critically: I found NO study applying a Reality Check or SPA test to a chart-PATTERN strategy (head and shoulders, flags, double tops) in FX at retail costs. The pattern-trading literature the user is drawing on has not been subjected to the multiple-testing discipline that the moving-average/filter-rule literature has.

**Survives retail costs?** 3% p.a. is after the managers' own trading costs but the funds charged fees on top of it. The relevant read for retail: the four things that explain professional FX returns are carry, trend, value and vol. None of them is a chart pattern. If a retail system's returns are not loading on those factors, the burden of proof is on the system.

**Sources:** <https://www.nber.org/papers/w13714>, <https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~rlevich/wp/PL1.pdf>, <https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/separating-alpha-and-beta-returns-are-currency-managers-measuring>, <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781118445785.ch17>

### Carry is the single largest systematic FX style, with a long-run Sharpe somewhere between 0.3 and 0.9 depending on construction and sample — and it is structurally negatively skewed, which is the textbook case of 'high win rate, catastrophic tail'.

**Quality:** mix: peer-reviewed (NBER Macro Annual; JIMF 2024) plus an NBER Reporter summary article — all read via search summary, primary text unreachable

**Evidence:** Menkhoff/Sarno/Schmeling/Schrimpf as summarised by the NBER Reporter: carry-trade average annualised payoff 4.5%, standard deviation 5.2%, Sharpe 0.86; currency momentum standard deviation 7.3%, Sharpe 0.60. Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen, 'Carry Trades and Currency Crashes' (NBER WP 14473, NBER Macro Annual 2008): carry portfolio annualised Sharpe 0.44 at weekly frequency and 0.41 at quarterly, with skewness of -0.41 weekly and -0.88 quarterly; negative skew arises from sudden unwinds when risk appetite and funding liquidity fall. A more recent out-of-sample study (Journal of International Money and Finance 2024, 'The out-of-sample performance of carry trades') tests 1,323 carry strategies (rising to 17,199 with learning and stop-loss variants) and reports Sharpe 0.79 before costs, 0.74 after proportional costs. A G10 carry basket is reported to have taken a 37% drawdown, with its all-time high in July 2007.

**Against:** Strong and multiple. (1) Daniel, Hodrick & Lu, 'The Carry Trade: Risks and Drawdowns' (Critical Finance Review 2017, 6: 211-262; NBER WP 20433) find DOLLAR-NEUTRAL G10 carry has INSIGNIFICANT abnormal returns once risk factors are accounted for, is highly negatively skewed and carries considerable downside risk; the return that survives is the dollar-exposure leg, not the classic high-minus-low-yield trade. (2) Practitioner research reports ~80% of carry's value generation happened in the 2000s pre-GFC with almost no positive PnL contribution since 2010, and a 20-year Sharpe of only 0.33 (onshore rate differentials) to 0.44 (forward-implied). (3) The published Sharpe range across sources here is 0.33 to 0.86 — a factor of 2.6 — which is itself a warning that construction choices are driving the answer.

**Survives retail costs?** Partially, and the distinction matters. Proportional bid-ask costs are small relative to carry's ~22-day average holding period: reported net Sharpe stays above 0.85 at 2bp per trade and ~0.60 even at 10bp per trade. But retail does NOT get interbank carry — retail swap/rollover is quoted with a broker markup on top of the interbank differential, and that markup is charged every night. A retail carry book is paying the true differential minus a spread on BOTH legs, which can consume most of the apparent carry. Also note: carry is the classic high-win-rate strategy — it pays a small positive amount most days and then loses years of it in one unwind. It is the living proof of rule 4.

**Sources:** <https://www.nber.org/reporter/2012number3/understanding-profitability-currency-trading-strategies>, <https://www.nber.org/papers/w14473>, <https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14473/w14473.pdf>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261560624000299>, <https://www.scu.edu/media/leavey-school-of-business/economics/wcwif/MELVIN--Shand_Carry-Trade.pdf>

### Transaction costs are first-order in FX and are the thing that actually decides whether any of this works — and the binding cost is different for funds (price impact) than for retail (spread + swap markup).

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Financial Economics 2024) for the price-impact result; industry/vendor broker-comparison data for the retail spread figures — all read via search summary

**Evidence:** Filippou, Maurer, Pezzo & Taylor, 'Importance of transaction costs for asset allocation in foreign exchange markets' (Journal of Financial Economics 2024, vol 159; CEPR DP19037). Finding: proportional costs from quoted bid-ask spreads are relatively small, BUT costs from trading-volume price impact are sizable and quickly erode returns, leaving popular strategies — carry, momentum, mean-variance-optimised currency portfolios — UNPROFITABLE once a fund reaches a certain size. Retail side of the ledger: industry-average EURUSD retail spread ~0.88 pips round-trip in 2026 across brokers (~$8.80 per standard lot), with typical retail quotes 0.8-1.0 pip, versus ~0.1 pip interbank-quoted; on top of that, brokers add their own markup to swap rates above the interbank differential.

**Against:** The same paper shows the problem is tractable: a mean-variance-transaction-cost (MVTC) optimisation that puts costs inside the objective function recovers most of the before-cost profitability. So 'costs kill it' is not the final word for a manager who designs around them — it is the final word for a manager who ignores them. Also, retail's constraint is the mirror image of the fund's: a retail account has literally zero price impact, so the capacity problem that stops a $10bn CTA does not apply. Retail's problem is the fixed ~0.9 pip toll per round trip, which is trivial for a 3-month holding period and fatal for an intraday one.

**Survives retail costs?** This is the cost claim. Concrete arithmetic the trader should run: 0.88 pips round-trip on EURUSD at ~1.10 is roughly 8 basis points per round trip. A monthly-horizon system (12 round trips/year) pays ~0.96% a year in spread — survivable against a 4-5% gross edge. A system taking 3 trades a day (~750/year) pays ~60% a year in spread alone. No chart pattern has a 60%-a-year edge. The horizon, not the pattern, decides whether costs are survivable.

**Sources:** <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X24001090>, <https://cepr.org/publications/dp19037>, <https://alphaarchitect.com/fx-markets/>, <https://forexspreadcompare.com/artigos/eurusd-spread-comparison>, <https://www.forex.com/en/about-us/financial-transparency/trading-costs/>, <https://www.forex.com/en/about-us/financial-transparency/rollover-rates/>

### Cross-sectional currency momentum shows a headline spread of up to 10% p.a., but the return is concentrated in exactly the currencies you cannot cheaply trade, and the authors themselves conclude it is not readily exploitable.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Financial Economics 2012), top-tier — read via search summary; I could not open the paper to extract the exact pre- vs post-cost return pair

**Evidence:** Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf, 'Currency momentum strategies' (Journal of Financial Economics 2012, 106(3), 660-684; CEPR DP8747). Reported: significant cross-sectional spread in excess returns of up to 10% per annum between past winner and past loser currencies; the spread is not explained by traditional risk factors. Momentum returns are 'fairly sensitive to transaction costs' and adjusting for bid-ask spreads lowers profitability significantly BECAUSE momentum portfolios are skewed towards currencies with high transaction costs — i.e. the signal loads on illiquid/EM crosses. Reported Sharpe ~0.60 with 7.3% volatility.

**Against:** The paper's own framing cuts both ways: transaction costs are 'unable to completely account for' the momentum returns, so there is residual return after spreads. But the authors then attribute the survival of that residual to 'very effective limits to arbitrage that prevent momentum returns from being easily exploitable' — which is an academic way of saying a retail trader cannot get it either. Filippou et al. (JFE 2024, above) independently find momentum specifically turns unprofitable once price impact is included.

**Survives retail costs?** Weakly and only at long horizons for a small account. Retail spreads on the EM crosses where this effect lives (TRY, ZAR, MXN, HUF) are often 10-50x the EURUSD spread, plus punitive swap markups, plus weekend gap risk. The effect is real in the data and largely unreachable in a retail account.

**Sources:** <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X12001353>, <https://cepr.org/publications/dp8747>, <https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/3296/>, <https://www.nber.org/reporter/2012number3/understanding-profitability-currency-trading-strategies>

### SHORT-HORIZON trend — which is the horizon retail FX education actually sells — has specifically decayed since 2008, and the decay is concentrated in small-tick contracts, which is exactly what FX majors are.

**Quality:** working paper, not peer-reviewed (arXiv 2607.01550) for the tick-size result; peer-reviewed (JFQA 2009) for the out-of-sample decay of FX technical rules. Both read via search summary. The arXiv paper is recent and unreplicated — treat the mechanism as a hypothesis.

**Evidence:** 'Is Trend Still Your Friend?: A Microstructural Account of the Demise of Short-Term Trend-Following' (arXiv 2607.01550, July 2026). Universe: ~100 liquid futures contracts, 1995-2025. Central finding: the cross-sectional variable separating degraded trends from surviving ones is the VOLATILITY-NORMALISED TICK SIZE. Post-2008, trend PnL has collapsed on small-tick contracts across ALL signal horizons, while remaining essentially intact on large-tick contracts. Mechanism proposed: trend's profitability rested on a self-fulfilling impact loop (signal triggers trade, trade's market impact reinforces the move), and the post-crisis shift to HFT-dominated market making — which withdraws liquidity ahead of predictable directional flow — broke that loop where the limit order book is sparse. Separately, Neely, Weller & Ulrich (Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 2009) run TRUE out-of-sample tests on previously published FX technical rules and find the 1970s-80s excess returns were genuine (not data-mined) but that filter-rule and moving-average-rule profit opportunities had DISAPPEARED by the early 1990s; less-studied rules declined but had probably not fully disappeared.

**Against:** Lempérière, Deremble, Seager, Potters & Bouchaud, 'Two centuries of trend following' (arXiv 1404.3274, CFM) establish an anomalous trend excess return across four asset classes INCLUDING CURRENCIES, using futures from 1960 and spot series back to 1800, with an aggregate Sharpe of 0.72 — trend as a long-run phenomenon is about as well-established as anything in finance. The two results are reconcilable (long-horizon trend survives, short-horizon trend on small-tick instruments died) but a reader should note they point opposite directions on the headline question 'does trend work'. The Neely/Weller/Ulrich result is also explicitly an ADAPTIVE-markets story: rules decay as they are published and crowded, which implies anything currently taught widely is the worst candidate.

**Survives retail costs?** No, at short horizons — and the arXiv paper's mechanism IS a cost mechanism. If HFT market makers pull liquidity ahead of predictable directional flow on small-tick instruments, the short-horizon trend follower's realised slippage rises exactly when the signal fires. That is adverse selection on top of the ~0.9 pip spread. The practical conclusion: the more intraday the system, the more of the edge is a transfer to the liquidity provider.

**Sources:** <https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01550>, <https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01550v1>, <https://quantpedia.com/is-trend-still-your-friend-a-microstructural-account-of-the-demise-of-short-term-trend-following/>, <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/adaptive-markets-hypothesis-evidence-from-the-foreign-exchange-market/9D336CDCA83233819EB5CDD0F4BC0DAA>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1403345>

### On chart patterns specifically — the one serious academic test of a named chart pattern in FX found mixed, currency-dependent results against a random-walk bootstrap, and it reports risk-adjusted excess profit, not a 'win rate'.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (The Economic Journal 1999) plus a Federal Reserve Bank of New York staff report — read via search summary only; I could not open either to get the per-currency numbers or the exact bootstrap p-values

**Evidence:** Chang & Osler, 'Methodical Madness: Technical Analysis and the Irrationality of Exchange-Rate Forecasts' (The Economic Journal 1999, 109(458)), plus the related NY Fed staff report 'Head and Shoulders: Not Just a Flaky Pattern'. Method: head-and-shoulders patterns identified by an objective computer algorithm coded from published technical-analysis manuals, applied to daily exchange rates of major currencies vs the dollar over the float, March 1973 to June 1994; resulting real-time-replicable profits compared against the distribution of profits from 10,000 bootstrap-simulated series under a random-walk null. Finding as summarised: evidence of excess returns for SOME currencies but not others.

**Against:** The 'some currencies but not others' result is itself the contradicting evidence, and it is the shape you expect from data snooping: test one pattern across ~6 currencies, get 2-3 significant, and the surviving ones are the ones the education industry quotes. Osler's companion equity work ('The Predictive Power of Head-and-Shoulders Price Patterns', Savin et al.) and the 'Can Technical Pattern Trading Be Profitably Automated?' literature reach less favourable conclusions about the pattern's standalone value versus simpler rules. To the paper's credit it DID apply a 10,000-path bootstrap reality check against a random-walk null — that is better data-snooping hygiene than 99% of what the retail industry cites — but it is still a single-pattern test on a 1973-1994 sample that ended 30 years ago.

**Survives retail costs?** Very doubtful today. The sample is 1973-1994, when the retail FX market did not exist and institutional spreads were several times wider than now; the relevant question is whether an effect measured against a random-walk null in that era survives a 0.88-pip retail toll in 2026 against HFT-provided liquidity. I found no study that tests head-and-shoulders in FX on post-2010 data with modern retail costs. Anyone selling a win rate for this pattern is not citing this paper.

**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1468-0297.00466>, <https://fedinprint.org/item/fednsr/12465>, <https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fednsr/4.html>, <https://www.biz.uiowa.edu/faculty/gsavin/papers/hsrevision_paw_10%2019%2006.pdf>, <https://www.statslab.cam.ac.uk/~mrt31//CFR/publications/content/papers/H&S-part2.pdf>

### There is direct academic evidence from real retail FX accounts that USING technical analysis is NEGATIVELY associated with performance — the opposite sign to what the retail education industry sells.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Portfolio Management; Journal of International Money and Finance) — read via search summary

**Evidence:** Abbey & Doukas, 'Is Technical Analysis Profitable for Individual Currency Traders?' (Journal of Portfolio Management 2012; see also Abbey's ODU dissertation 'Three Essays on Individual Currency Traders'). Data: proprietary database of 428 individual currency traders from an online advisory service, daily returns, March 2004 to September 2009, testing four popular technical indicators. Findings: (a) traders as a group earned positive excess returns even after transaction costs; (b) the USE of technical analysis was NEGATIVELY associated with performance; (c) Bollinger Bands were the single most-favoured indicator in the sample, beating RSI, MACD and moving-average crossovers for popularity — and the traders who used Bollinger Bands UNDERPERFORMED their peers who did not. A related Abbey & Doukas paper ('Do individual currency traders make money?', Journal of International Money and Finance 2015) finds no evidence that retail FX traders learn to trade better, though they do learn about their own innate ability and respond appropriately.

**Against:** Serious sample-selection problem: 428 traders from a paid online advisory service are not representative of retail FX, and the finding that this group earned POSITIVE excess returns after costs sits badly against the regulator-mandated broker disclosures showing 65-89% of retail CFD accounts losing money. Either the advisory-service population is unusually skilled, or the sample/period (which spans the 2008 volatility spike, a good period for directional FX) is not general. The correlation between TA use and underperformance is also not identified as causal — worse traders may adopt TA rather than TA making traders worse.

**Survives retail costs?** The headline result is explicitly stated as after transaction costs, which is the right test. But the cost base in 2004-2009 retail FX was wider than today's, so the direction of bias on the positive-excess-return finding is unclear.

**Sources:** <https://efmaefm.org/0doukas/publications/pdf/IsTechnicalAnalysisProfitable_12-14-2011.pdf>, <https://www.proquest.com/docview/1356625786>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261560614001624>, <https://digitalcommons.odu.edu/businessadministration_etds/6/>

### Win rate and profitability are empirically decoupled in retail FX: the largest public dataset of real retail trades shows traders winning MORE than half their trades and still losing money, because losses were roughly twice the size of wins.

**Quality:** industry / broker research for the 43m-trade study (FXCM has a commercial interest in the 'cut your losses' conclusion, and the raw data and methodology were never published or peer-reviewed); vendor/blog for the 35-45% institutional hit-rate figure, which I could NOT trace to any fund's audited disclosure

**Evidence:** DailyFX/FXCM research on over 43 million real trades placed on FXCM servers, Q2 2014 to Q1 2015. Findings: traders closed over 50% of trades at a gain in most major pairs, yet the majority of traders still lost money overall, because average losses were far larger than average gains. The worked example given: in GBP/USD the average winning trade was 43 pips while the average losing trade was 83 pips — a 0.52 payoff ratio. Even at a 60% win rate, expectancy = 0.60(43) - 0.40(83) = 25.8 - 33.2 = -7.4 pips per trade BEFORE spread. Contrast the institutional design: professional trend-following programs are commonly described as running 35-45% hit rates with deliberately asymmetric payoffs, and Hamill, Rattray & Van Hemert characterise trend following as positively skewed — many small losses, occasional large winners.

**Against:** The study is broker marketing research, not independent work: no published methodology, no confidence intervals, no control for account size or survivorship, and the conclusion it draws ('exit winners too early') happens to encourage clients to hold positions longer, which generates more swap revenue for the broker. The specific 43-vs-83 pip figure should be treated as illustrative, not as a measured population parameter. I could not find a peer-reviewed replication on retail FX tick data. The 35-45% professional hit rate is also, as far as I could verify, folklore repeated across vendor blogs rather than a disclosed statistic.

**Survives retail costs?** The FXCM figures ARE realised client P&L, so they are net of that broker's spread — which makes them the most cost-honest retail number in this report, and they are negative. Add the spread explicitly and the picture worsens: at ~0.9 pips round-trip, the GBPUSD example's expectancy falls to roughly -8.3 pips per trade. This is the direct, empirical answer to the trader's premise: a high win rate in retail FX is the single most reliable observed predictor of a losing account, because high win rates are manufactured by taking small profits and holding losers.

**Sources:** <https://www.fxstreet.com/analysis/fundamental-forex-commentary/2015/06/26>, <https://www.moneyshow.com/articles/currency-42927/>, <https://learnpriceaction.com/why-traders-lose-money-and-the-forex-pairs-they-win-the-most-on/>, <https://www.quantifiedstrategies.com/what-is-the-success-rate-of-trend-following-trading-strategies/>

### The regulator-mandated loss disclosures put the retail base rate at roughly 65-89% of accounts losing money, and it has not improved in the eight years since disclosure became mandatory.

**Quality:** regulatory for the ESMA/CFTC figures and the broker disclosures (these are legally mandated and auditable — the strongest data in this report); industry/vendor aggregation for the 49-broker average and for all prop-firm statistics (prop-firm numbers come from firms and affiliate sites with an incentive to publish favourable pass rates — treat as weak)

### The horizon and capital gap is not a matter of degree — the institutional FX market is a funding and hedging market, and the directional systematic business is a small, long-horizon slice of it.

**Quality:** authoritative/official for the BIS turnover data (BIS Triennial Survey, corroborated by RBA and HKMA national releases) — read via search summary; industry/vendor for the CTA leverage, vol-target and holding-period figures

**Evidence:** BIS Triennial Central Bank Survey, April 2025: global FX turnover reached a record $9.51 trillion per day. Spot accounted for 31% of turnover (up from 28% in 2022); FX SWAPS remained the single most-traded instrument at ~$4 trillion/day, roughly 42% of the total. So the majority of the $9.5tn is rolling funding and hedging flow, not directional speculation. On the capital/leverage side: CTAs run notional leverage of roughly 3:1 to 8:1 with initial margin at 2-10% of notional, targeting ~10% annualised portfolio volatility and scaling positions inversely to each market's recent volatility. Retail CFD leverage is capped at 30:1 on majors in the EU/UK and is commonly 100:1 to 500:1 offshore. Horizon: global macro themes typically run days to weeks to months, with a typical macro FX trade expressing a view over the next ~12 months; CTA trend signals are 1-12 months.

**Against:** Not all institutional FX is slow: the survey also shows spot's share RISING (28%→31%), and some systematic macro strategies are described as running shorter holding periods than trend following, with high position turnover. There are genuine intraday and HFT FX businesses — but they are market-making and latency businesses competing on infrastructure, which is the one game a retail trader definitively cannot enter. The 'institutions are slow' framing should not be overstated; the accurate framing is that institutions are either much slower than retail or vastly faster, and almost nothing profitable lives in the retail middle.

**Survives retail costs?** The structural point is a cost point. A 10%-vol, 3-8x levered, 60-market portfolio rebalanced monthly pays a few basis points a year in execution. A 100:1 levered single-pair intraday account pays ~8bp per round trip. Same signal, two orders of magnitude difference in cost drag. That gap, not pattern selection, is the main reason the two populations have different outcomes.

### The correct summary contrast for this trader: institutional systematic FX optimises expectancy per unit of cost at monthly horizons across many markets and accepts a low win rate; retail FX education optimises win rate at intraday horizons on one or two pairs and ignores the cost base. **Evidence:** Assembling the figures above: best-known institutional trend benchmark ~4.9% p.a. / Sharpe ~0.5 net since 2000, with a decade at 0.4% p.a. against a 21.8% drawdown; average CTA net excess return over T-bills 1.8% p.a., statistically zero; professional FX managers ~3% p.a. gross of fees, ~76% with no significant alpha; carry Sharpe 0.33-0.86 depending on construction with -0.88 quarterly skew; currency momentum ~10% p.a. gross concentrated in untradeable crosses; short-horizon trend PnL collapsed post-2008 on small-tick instruments; 65-89% of retail accounts losing; and the only large retail dataset showing >50% win rates coexisting with net losses at a 43:83 pip payoff ratio.

## What a retail FX system actually pays — and the break-even edge that kills most of them.

METHOD CAVEAT, READ FIRST: in this environment WebFetch was blocked by the egress proxy for every domain I tried (esma.europa.eu, bis.org, sciencedirect, ssrn, nber, cftc.gov, fca.org.uk, arxiv, escholarship, wikipedia, cmegroup, investing.com — all 403 policy denials). I could run WebSearch but could NOT open a single primary document. Every external number below comes from search-engine summaries of real, citable pages, not from my own reading of the source text. I have labelled quality accordingly and flagged which figures I could not verify. Treat any number tagged "unverified-summary" as needing a second look before it goes in a spec.

HEADLINE ANSWER (this part is arithmetic, not literature, and is the reliable part):

1. Round-turn cost on EURUSD at a realistic retail broker is 0.9–2.5 pips. Raw-spread and standard accounts converge: raw = ~0.2–0.4 spread + 0.7 commission ($3.50/side/lot) + ~0.3 slippage ≈ 1.2–1.5; standard = ~1.2 spread + ~0.3 slippage ≈ 1.5. Offshore/wide ≈ 2.5. News windows 5+.

2. THE FORMULA THAT MATTERS. For a fixed target W and stop L (pips) and round-turn cost c:
   break-even win rate p* = (L + c) / (W + L)
   cost penalty over the zero-cost break-even = c / (W + L)
   The cost hurdle scales inversely with (W + L) — the TOTAL pips at stake per trade. This is the single most important fact for someone hunting "highest win rate" patterns, because high-win-rate designs are exactly the ones with small W, and small W means c is a large fraction of every winner.

   At c = 1.5 pips:
   - 5 pip target / 5 pip stop: need 65.0% (vs 50.0% free) — +15.0pp
   - 10 / 10: need 57.5% — +7.5pp
   - 20 / 20: need 53.8% — +3.8pp
   - 50 / 50: need 51.5% — +1.5pp
   - 100 / 50: need 34.3% (vs 33.3%) — +1.0pp
   - 5 / 45 ("90% win rate" design): need 93.0% (vs 90.0%) — +3.0pp
   - 3 / 30: need 95.5% (vs 90.9%) — +4.5pp
   At c = 2.5 pips a 5/5 scalp needs 75% just to break even.

3. THE NUMBER THAT KILLS SYSTEMS — annual friction as % of ACCOUNT EQUITY (EURUSD; 1 pip = 0.909 bp of notional at 1.10; leverage = average open notional / equity):
   c = 1.5 pips:
     250 trades/yr: 3.4% @1:1, 10.2% @3:1, 17.0% @5:1, 34.1% @10:1, 102.3% @30:1
     500 trades/yr: 6.8% @1:1, 20.5% @3:1, 34.1% @5:1, 68.2% @10:1, 204.5% @30:1
    1000 trades/yr: 13.6% @1:1, 40.9% @3:1, 68.2% @5:1, 136.4% @10:1, 409.1% @30:1
   Anchor: a disciplined trader risking 1% with a 30-pip stop runs ~3:1 effective leverage. 250 trades/yr at 1.5 pips = ~10% of equity/yr, i.e. ~375 pips of GROSS edge needed annually before a cent of profit. At 5 trades/day (1,250/yr) that becomes ~50% of equity/yr at 3:1.

4. Pips are not comparable across pairs — convert to bps of notional: EURUSD@1.10 1 pip = 0.909 bp; GBPUSD@1.27 = 0.787 bp; USDJPY@150 = 0.667 bp. A "1.2 pip" GBPUSD spread is cheaper in real terms than a "1.2 pip" EURUSD spread, and a "1.5 pip" USDJPY spread at 150 is cheaper still. Backtests that treat a pip as a constant cost across pairs are wrong in both directions.

5. ESMA 30:1 does not reduce cost per pip — it reduces size, and therefore both return and cost as % of equity, proportionally. It changes nothing about break-even edge per trade. What it does change: at maximum 30:1 with the 50%-of-initial-margin close-out, forced liquidation comes after an adverse move of 1.667% of notional = ~183 pips EURUSD, ~212 pips GBPUSD, ~250 pips USDJPY.

BOTTOM LINE FOR THE USER'S ACTUAL QUESTION: a "high win rate" pattern is the worst possible thing to build a retail FX system around, because high win rates are bought with small targets and wide stops, and the cost hurdle c/(W+L) is at its most punishing when W is small and turnover is high. The cost-efficient design is the opposite: fewer trades, larger (W+L), payoff ratio above 1. Any pattern with a target under ~20 pips is paying 4–15 percentage points of win rate to the broker before the pattern has done anything.

### Realistic all-in round-turn cost on EURUSD at a retail broker is 0.9-2.5 pips, and raw-spread vs standard accounts converge to roughly the same number once commission and slippage are included.

**Quality:** vendor/blog (affiliate broker-comparison sites) combined with my own arithmetic. I could not retrieve any regulator- or exchange-published retail spread measurement — every primary domain was blocked.

**Evidence:** Commission on a raw/ECN account of ~$3.50 per side per standard lot = $7 round turn = 0.70 pips on EURUSD (1 lot = $10/pip). Search-surfaced broker comparison data: raw accounts quote EURUSD 0.0-0.1 pips with $2.25/side ($4.50 RT = 0.45 pips); standard/market-maker accounts 1.0-1.2 pips fixed, up to 1.0-3.0. Slippage 0.0-0.3 pips is described as 'excellent' for ECN, 0.5-1.0 as 'good' for standard. My scenario arithmetic: raw = 0.2-0.4 spread + 0.7 commission + 0.3-0.4 slip = 1.2-1.5 pips ($12-15/lot); standard = 1.2 spread + 0.3 slip = 1.5 pips; offshore/wide = 1.8 + 0.7 = 2.5 pips; news/rollover hours = 3.0 + 2.0 = 5.0 pips.

The 0.9-2.5 range is a range, not a point estimate, and I could not verify either end against a neutral source.

**Survives retail costs?** N/A - this IS the cost. Expressed in bps of notional: 0.9 pips = 0.82 bp, 1.5 pips = 1.36 bp, 2.5 pips = 2.27 bp per round turn on EURUSD.

**Sources:** <https://www.compareforexbrokers.com/our-methodology/forex-fees/tightest-spreads/>, <https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/analysis/how-do-the-costs-of-retail-forex-trading-compare/>, <https://www.fxcm.com/markets/execution/slippage-statistics/>, <https://newyorkcityservers.com/blog/lowest-spread-forex-brokers-2026>

### Transaction cost raises the required win rate by exactly c/(W+L), so the penalty is largest for exactly the small-target, high-win-rate systems the trader is asking for.

**Quality:** model-knowledge-only (my own arithmetic). It is algebra, not an empirical finding, so it cannot be wrong - but it can be misapplied.

**Evidence:** Deterministic algebra. Net expectancy = pW - (1-p)L - c = 0 gives p* = (L+c)/(W+L); zero-cost break-even p0 = L/(W+L); difference = c/(W+L). At c=1.5 pips: 5/5 scalp needs 65.0% vs 50.0% (+15.0pp); 10/10 needs 57.5% (+7.5pp); 10/20 needs 71.7% vs 66.7% (+5.0pp); 10/50 needs 85.8% vs 83.3% (+2.5pp); 20/20 needs 53.8% (+3.8pp); 50/50 needs 51.5% (+1.5pp); 100/50 needs 34.3% vs 33.3% (+1.0pp); a 3-pip-target/30-pip-stop '90.9% win rate' design needs 95.5% (+4.5pp). At c=2.5 pips the 5/5 scalp needs 75.0%.

**Against:** Nothing contradicts the algebra, but three things weaken it as a model of a real system, and they all make the true hurdle WORSE, not better: (1) it assumes one entry and one exit - scaling in/out, re-entries, or a trailing stop that gets re-hit multiply c by the number of round turns; (2) it assumes W and L are the fixed target and stop, whereas real stops slip through and real targets sometimes fill partially, widening L and shrinking W; (3) it ignores swap on any trade held overnight (see separate claim). I found no source that disputes it and I did not look for one, because a formula does not need corroboration - but note per the brief that absence of contradiction here is trivially explained, not evidence of strength.

**Survives retail costs?** This formula IS the survival test. Rule of thumb it produces: any FX system whose target is under ~20 pips is handing 4-15 percentage points of win rate to the broker before the entry signal has done any work.

### A published retail broker sample shows a 61% win rate on EUR/USD that is still a losing strategy, because the average loser (83 pips) is 73% bigger than the average winner (48 pips).

**Quality:** vendor (broker marketing/education content). This is a broker publishing statistics about its own clients, in a document whose purpose is to sell a trading behaviour. I could not open the PDF to check sample size, period, pair coverage, or methodology - the search summary gave the numbers without them.

**Evidence:** FXCM/DailyFX 'Traits of Successful Traders': EUR/USD trades closed at a profit 61% of the time; average winning trade 48 pips, average losing trade 83 pips; 'traders lost 70% more on their losing trades than they won on winning trades'. Payoff ratio W/L = 0.58. Expectancy = 0.61(48) - 0.39(83) = 29.28 - 32.37 = -3.09 pips per trade. Break-even win rate at that payoff ratio is 63.4%. This is the cleanest published demonstration that win rate is not edge.

**Against:** Several real problems with leaning on this: (1) single broker, single client base, self-selected, no disclosed sample size or date range in anything I could reach; (2) the numbers are almost certainly ALREADY NET of spread, since FXCM's retail model at the time was spread-only with no commission - so you must NOT subtract c again on top of -3.09, that would double-count; the gross figure would be roughly -1.6 pips, still negative; (3) it is a cross-sectional average over heterogeneous traders, so it says nothing about whether any individual rule has an edge; (4) Abbey & Doukas (JIMF 2015, n=428) found the opposite direction for a subsample - 'traders are able to earn positive excess returns, even after accounting for transaction costs' - though that sample is restricted to traders profitable enough that others copy them, which is survivorship selection so severe the result is near-uninterpretable for this question.

**Survives retail costs?** No. It is negative before you add any further cost. And the direction of the effect is the point: the trader's stated goal (maximise win rate) is precisely the behaviour that produced 61% wins and a negative expectancy.

**Sources:** <https://www.fxcm.com/markets/trading-guides/traits-successful-traders/>, <https://docs.fxcorporate.com/fxcm-traits-of-successful-traders-guide.pdf>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261560614001624>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2514514>

### Annual friction, not any single trade's spread, is what kills retail systems: at realistic leverage and turnover it consumes 10-200% of account equity per year.

**Quality:** model-knowledge-only for the arithmetic; regulator (AMF, French securities supervisor) for the turnover-loss relationship, but retrieved only as a search summary quoted via BaFin's administrative act - I could not open the AMF study itself.

**Evidence:** My arithmetic, EURUSD, 1 pip = 0.909 bp of notional at 1.10. Annual drag = c(pips) x 0.909bp x trades/yr x effective leverage. At c=1.5 pips: 250 trades/yr costs 3.4% of equity at 1:1, 10.2% at 3:1, 17.0% at 5:1, 34.1% at 10:1, 102.3% at 30:1. At 500 trades/yr: 6.8/20.5/34.1/68.2/204.5%. At 1,000 trades/yr: 13.6/40.9/68.2/136.4/409.1%. Practical anchor: risking 1% of equity per trade with a 30-pip stop implies ~3.3:1 effective leverage, so 250 trades/yr = ~10% of equity/yr = ~375 pips of gross edge needed annually to break even. Corroborating direction from a regulator: the AMF's 2014 study of 14,799 French CFD/forex clients (2009-2013) found 'the more transactions a client makes, the higher their loss is'.

**Against:** The leverage multiplier cuts both ways and I should not overstate it: leverage scales gross returns by exactly the same factor it scales costs, so leverage alone does not change the break-even EDGE per trade - it changes variance and ruin probability. The honest version of this claim is: turnover is the multiplier that matters for the cost/edge ratio; leverage is the multiplier that matters for how fast the cost shows up as a percentage of equity and how likely you are to be closed out before the edge pays. An unsourced claim I saw ('overtrading transaction costs erode account equity by 15-30% per year in many published studies') appeared only on a vendor blog and I could not trace it to any study - do not cite it, though note it happens to land inside my computed 3:1-to-5:1 range.

**Survives retail costs?** This IS the cost, annualised. The decision rule it implies: cut trades/year before you cut spread. Halving turnover halves friction; moving from a 1.5-pip to a 1.2-pip broker cuts it by 20%.

**Sources:** <https://www.bafin.de/SharedDocs/Veroeffentlichungen/EN/Aufsichtsrecht/Verfuegung/vf_170508_allgvfg_cfd_wa_en.html>, <https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/contenu_simple/rapport_etude_analyse/epargne_prestataire/Etude%20des%20resultats%20des%20investisseurs%20particuliers%20sur%20le%20trading%20de%20CFD%20et%20de%20Forex%20en%20France.pdf>

### Swap/rollover carries a broker markup of roughly 0.5-3% per year charged on NOTIONAL in both directions, which at high leverage is a larger annual cost than the spread for any position held more than a few days.

**Quality:** vendor/broker fee disclosure (factual schedule rather than marketing claim, which makes it more trustworthy than a broker performance claim, but it is still self-reported and unaudited). Search summaries only - I could not open the rate cards.

**Evidence:** Broker fee disclosures: financing set at 'benchmark regional interest rate +/- 2.5%' (FOREX.com); 'many brokers add a small admin markup (for example +2% or +3% above the benchmark)'; 'broker markup of typically 0.5-2% annualised' on top of the interest differential. Converting to pips per night per standard lot on EURUSD at 1.10: 0.5%/yr = 0.151 pips/night ($1.51); 1.0% = 0.301 pips ($3.01); 2.5% = 0.753 pips ($7.53); 3.0% = 0.904 pips ($9.04). Wednesday is charged triple, so a Monday-to-Friday hold is 6 swap days, not 4 - at a 2.5% markup that is ~4.5 pips of markup alone, three times a typical EURUSD round-turn spread cost. As % of equity per year if continuously held: a 1%/yr markup = 5% at 5:1, 10% at 10:1, 30% at 30:1; a 2.5%/yr markup = 12.5% at 5:1, 25% at 10:1, 75% at 30:1.

**Against:** The markup is not the whole swap and the net can be a credit. The swap is (interest differential) +/- (markup), so on the carry-favourable side of a wide differential you can receive net positive swap even after the markup - holding long USD/JPY during a wide US-Japan differential, for example, has paid retail traders. So 'swap is a cost' is false as a blanket statement; 'the markup is always against you, in both directions' is the defensible version. Second contradiction: brokers vary enormously and some triple-swap conventions fall on different days - I found no independent audit of any broker's actual applied markup vs its published one, and I could not verify current benchmark rates, so these are illustrative conversions, not a current quote.

**Survives retail costs?** Decisive for holding period. A swing system holding 5 nights pays ~3.8 pips of markup at 2.5%/yr on top of ~1.5 pips of round-turn cost: total ~5.3 pips. A day-trading system pays 1.5 and nothing overnight. This reverses the usual advice - overnight holding is not free just because you trade less often.

**Sources:** <https://www.forex.com/en-uk/about-us/financial-transparency/trading-costs-charges/>, <https://www.forex.com/en/about-us/financial-transparency/rollover-rates/>, <https://www.activtrades.com/en/news/what-are-swap-rates-overnight-financing-for-cfd-positions>

### Published broker slippage data does NOT support the folklore that retail stops are systematically slipped against you under normal conditions - but it also does not test what matters, because it aggregates stops with limits.

**Quality:** vendor/broker execution disclosure (FXCM's own page, published under a best-execution regime but not independently audited) plus industry blog for the Pepperstone/IC Markets figures.

**Evidence:** FXCM's published execution statistics: 30.83% of all stop, limit, 'at market' and entry orders received POSITIVE slippage versus 14.64% negative, for orders executed through Stratos Group 1 Jan - 30 Nov 2025. Industry reporting describes 20-30% positive slippage at Pepperstone and IC Markets during normal hours. Typical magnitudes quoted: 0.0-0.3 pips for ECN, 0.5-1.0 for standard, 5-50 pips during news.

**Against:** This is the claim in my set where the evidence cuts AGAINST the intuitive story, and I am flagging that deliberately. Three caveats that pull back the other way: (1) the 30.83/14.64 split lumps limit orders - which get positive slippage almost by construction when price gaps through a target - together with stops, which is the population that matters for a stop-loss system; no broker I found publishes the stop-only split, and that omission is itself informative; (2) it is a dealer publishing statistics about its own fills; (3) the same industry sources report that on 'many offshore MT4/MT5 brokers' traders observe '100% negative slippage, 0% positive' - unquantified forum-grade evidence, but it means the FXCM number is not transferable to an arbitrary broker. Net: budget 0.3 pips average slippage at a regulated broker, and do not assume the average is where your risk lives.

### Stop-loss orders cluster just beyond round numbers, which means retail stops sit exactly where cascades are most likely - a structural reason stop fills are worse than the average slippage statistic implies.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Finance; JIMF). Abstract/summary only - I could not open either paper, so I have the qualitative findings and the citations but not the effect magnitudes.

**Evidence:** Osler (2003), 'Currency Orders and Exchange Rate Dynamics: An Explanation for the Predictive Success of Technical Analysis', Journal of Finance 58(5):1791-1819, using the first available data on individual currency stop-loss and take-profit orders: take-profit orders cluster strongly AT round numbers, stop-loss orders cluster strongly JUST BEYOND round numbers. Follow-up: Osler, 'Stop-loss orders and price cascades in currency markets', Journal of International Money and Finance 24(2):219-241 (2005). The mechanism explains both technical-analysis predictions - reversals at support/resistance, and unusually rapid moves once those levels break.

**Against:** This finding is double-edged and the trader should understand both edges. It is the single best academic explanation for WHY support/resistance patterns have predictive content - so it argues FOR technical levels having real information. But the same mechanism means the price acceleration through a level is a cascade of other people's stops, which is precisely when your own stop, placed in the same cluster, fills badly. Further caveats: the order data is from a single bank around 1999-2000; the FX market has since been electronified, internalised, and algo-mediated, and I found no replication on modern data. A widely publicised microstructure regularity is also exactly the kind of thing that gets arbitraged away - which is the Neely/Weller/Ulrich pattern described below.

**Survives retail costs?** Partially, and asymmetrically. It argues that placing stops a few pips INSIDE the round-number cluster, or using wider stops away from obvious levels, is a real cost saving - but it also means any backtest that assumes stops fill at the stop price is optimistic by an unknown amount at exactly the moments that matter most.

**Sources:** <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1540-6261.00588>, <https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v58y2003i5p1791-1819.html>, <https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf>, <https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jimfin/v24y2005i2p219-241.html>

### Stops do not cap loss across gaps and flash events; the tail is orders of magnitude larger than the modelled cost, though EU/UK negative balance protection now caps it at the account balance.

**Quality:** central bank (Reserve Bank of Australia) for the yen flash event - the strongest non-paywalled source in this set, though again summary-only; industry press (Finance Magnates, Forbes, LeapRate) for the 2015 CHF broker-loss figures.

**Evidence:** RBA Statement on Monetary Policy Feb 2019, Box B: on 3 January 2019 the yen appreciated 3% against the USD in about 30 seconds, in the absence of material news, amid an order imbalance; stop-loss triggers by carry traders activated and amplified the move. SNB/EUR-CHF, 15 January 2015: FXCM clients alone sustained $225 million in NEGATIVE balances (debt beyond account equity), forcing ~$300m emergency financing; industry-wide broker losses estimated at over $1 billion, with multiple brokers defaulting or being absorbed. Magnitude context: a 3% move on USD/JPY at 150 is 450 pips - roughly 300x a 1.5-pip round-turn cost, and well past the ~250-pip ESMA close-out distance at maximum leverage.

**Against:** The tail has been materially truncated for EU/UK retail since 2018: ESMA's intervention mandated negative balance protection on a per-account basis, so a retail client's loss is now capped at account equity rather than going negative - the $225m-in-client-debt scenario cannot recur for an ESMA/FCA-regulated retail account. It CAN still recur at offshore brokers. Also honest: these are two events in a decade; over the vast majority of trades gap risk is zero, so including a large per-trade gap charge in a cost model would be wrong. The correct treatment is a separate ruin-probability term, not an addition to c.

**Survives retail costs?** No cost model survives this, which is the point - it is not a cost, it is a truncation of the return distribution. Practical consequence: position sizing must assume the stop does not hold, and weekend/holiday-session exposure (Japanese holiday weeks, 3 January-type Asian opens) must be sized separately from weekday exposure.

**Sources:** <https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/smp/2019/feb/box-b-the-recent-japanese-yen-flash-event.html>, <https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/brokers/fxcm-publishes-data-of-snb-mishandling-of-the-swiss-franc/>, <https://www.forbes.com/sites/steveschaefer/2015/01/16/swiss-bank-stunner-claims-victims-currency-broker-fxcm-bludgeoned/>, <https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/brokers/exclusive-japanese-brokers-face-8-6m-loss-due-to-january-flash-crash/>

### Leverage caps like ESMA's 30:1 work by making people trade less, not by making trading cheaper - brokers did not widen spreads in response, and the cap does not change the break-even edge per trade.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JFE 2019) for the causal estimates; the margin-distance figures are my arithmetic from ESMA's published rule parameters (30:1 major FX, 50% margin close-out).

**Evidence:** Heimer & Simsek, 'Should retail investors' leverage be limited?', Journal of Financial Economics 132(3), studying the 2010 CFTC cap in the US retail FX market with three datasets and a difference-in-differences design: the leverage constraint reduced trading volume by 23%, alleviated high-leverage traders' losses by 40%, and reduced brokerages' operating capital by 25%, while NOT affecting the relative bid-ask prices charged by brokerages. Mechanically, under ESMA's 30:1 plus the 50%-of-initial-margin close-out rule, initial margin is 3.33% of notional, close-out triggers at 1.67%, so a fully-levered position is force-closed after a 1.667% adverse move = ~183 pips EURUSD, ~212 pips GBPUSD, ~250 pips USDJPY.

**Against:** The cap has not made retail traders profitable in aggregate, which is the relevant test for this trader. ESMA's own pre-intervention finding was that 74-89% of retail CFD accounts lose money with average losses of EUR 1,600-29,000; industry aggregations of the mandated post-2018 risk-warning disclosures report loss rates still in the 65-84% band, with one aggregator claiming they rose from 76% to 84% over 2018-2024 and another reporting 71.0% across 49 brokers. Those aggregator figures are vendor-grade and I could not verify them; but even ESMA's own renewal decisions do not claim the loss rate collapsed. The 40% loss reduction in Heimer & Simsek is also specific to HIGH-leverage traders under the US 2010 cap, not a general claim about all retail, and the US cap (100:1 to 50:1) is a much weaker constraint than ESMA's 30:1.

**Survives retail costs?** The cap is neutral-to-helpful on costs: it forces smaller size, which proportionally shrinks the absolute cost paid, without the broker recouping it through wider spreads. But it does nothing for edge-per-trade, and it makes tight-stop strategies infeasible to size up, which pushes retail toward the small-target high-win-rate designs that have the worst cost/edge ratio.

### Regulator-collected data consistently shows 74-89% of retail CFD/FX accounts lose money, and the strongest single academic reading of retail FX outcomes is that the group performs no better than random trading once the spread is deducted.

**Quality:** Regulator/supervisory (ESMA, FCA, AMF, CFTC) - the highest-quality sources available on this question, but ALL retrieved as search summaries only; I could not open a single one of these PDFs. The Davison paper is a working paper (SSRN/institutional repository), not peer-reviewed, and its repository PDF was blocked.

**Evidence:** ESMA 2018 product intervention analysis: 74-89% of retail client accounts lose, average loss per client EUR 1,600-29,000. FCA CP16/40 (2016): 82% of a representative sample of CFD client accounts lost money, average outcome a loss of GBP 2,200. AMF (France, 2014): 89% of 14,799 active CFD/forex clients lost over 2009-2013, mean loss EUR 10,887, median EUR 1,843, aggregate EUR 161,115,493. CFTC-mandated quarterly RFED disclosures: roughly one-third of US retail forex accounts profitable, two-thirds unprofitable in a given quarter (a quarterly figure, so not comparable to the annual European numbers). Davison, 'The Retail FX Trader: Random Trading and the Negative Sum Game' (SSRN 2711214 / Nottingham Trent): finds a strong correlation between overall trader profitability and the impact of the bid-ask spread cost, and little if any evidence that retail FX traders as a group achieve results better than random trading; ~20% end with a profitable account, ~40% get margin-called.

**Against:** Real contradictions worth stating: (1) the CFTC figure is quarterly, so a 33% quarterly profit rate is not the complement of an 82% annual loss rate - these numbers get illegitimately blended in retail commentary and I nearly did it too; (2) the loss rates are for CFD accounts broadly, not FX-only, and equity/index/crypto CFDs may dominate some samples; (3) Abbey & Doukas (JIMF 2015) found positive excess returns net of costs in a 428-trader sample, directly contradicting the 'nobody makes money' reading - though that sample is restricted to traders whose signals others pay to copy, which is about as biased a selection as exists; (4) all of these include unrealised P&L and account-level rather than strategy-level outcomes, and none isolates a systematic rule-based trader from a discretionary gambler. The finding that survives all of this is the one directly on-angle: Davison's correlation between profitability and spread impact, and the AMF's finding that loss size rises with transaction count.

**Survives retail costs?** This IS the cost result. Two independent regulator datasets and one working paper all point at trading frequency / spread impact as the mechanism, not directional skill.

**Sources:** <https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/esma50-162-215_product_intervention_analysis_cfds.pdf>, <https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/consultation/cp16-40.pdf>, <https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-proposes-stricter-rules-contract-difference-products>, <https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/contenu_simple/rapport_etude_analyse/epargne_prestataire/Etude%20des%20resultats%20des%20investisseurs%20particuliers%20sur%20le%20trading%20de%20CFD%20et%20de%20Forex%20en%20France.pdf>, <https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=2711214>, <https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/26837/>, <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/chapter-I/part-5>

### The leading peer-reviewed paper on FX transaction costs killing strategies does NOT apply to a retail trader, and citing it for retail is a category error.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JFE 2024). Abstract/summary only; I could not open the paper, so I do not have their cost magnitudes in bps or their fund-size thresholds.

**Evidence:** Filippou, Maurer, Pezzo & Taylor, 'Importance of transaction costs for asset allocation in foreign exchange markets', Journal of Financial Economics 159 (2024): proportional costs based on quoted bid-ask spreads are 'relatively small'; it is costs from TRADING VOLUME PRICE IMPACT, when the fund is large, that quickly erode returns and render carry, momentum and mean-variance-optimised currency portfolios unprofitable. Their fix (MVTC - cost-aware optimisation) is a portfolio-construction remedy for size, not for spread.

**Against:** This is the claim I am most keen to flag as cutting against the naive reading. A retail trader trading 0.1-1 standard lots has literally zero price impact in a market whose daily turnover is measured in trillions - so the paper's headline cost driver does not touch them, and the paper's assessment of the cost component that DOES touch them (quoted spreads) is 'relatively small'. If anything this paper is evidence that a small trader faces a LOWER cost barrier than a fund on the same strategy. The counter-counter-point, and why my headline arithmetic still stands: the paper measures institutional costs at interbank spreads, whereas retail pays a marked-up spread plus commission plus a swap markup plus slippage on a far smaller ticket, and trades 10-100x more often per unit of capital. The retail problem is a fixed per-trade toll amplified by turnover, not price impact. These are different cost structures and the literature on one does not transfer to the other.

**Survives retail costs?** Their finding does not transfer. Do not use 'even carry and momentum die after costs in the JFE' as an argument about a retail system - it is about fund capacity.

**Sources:** <https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v159y2024ics0304405x24001090.html>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3143970>, <https://cepr.org/publications/dp19037>, <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X24001090>

### The FX technical-rule literature has been through formal data-snooping tests, and the verdict is that documented rule profits were real in the 1970s-80s and were gone by the early 1990s - so any backtested win rate on modern data should be assumed snooped until proven otherwise.

**Quality:** peer-reviewed (JFQA 2009; Journal of Finance 1999). Summaries only; the Sullivan-Timmermann-White PDF on kevinsheppard.com was the one full text I located a link to but could not fetch.

**Evidence:** Neely, Weller & Ulrich, 'The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis: Evidence from the Foreign Exchange Market', Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis (2009, FRB St Louis WP 2006-046): true out-of-sample tests of previously studied rules find the excess returns of the 1970s and 1980s were genuine and not data-mined, but the profit opportunities had disappeared by the early 1990s for filter and moving-average rules. Sullivan, Timmermann & White, 'Data-Snooping, Technical Trading Rule Performance, and the Bootstrap', Journal of Finance 54(5):1647-1691 (1999): applies White's Reality Check bootstrap over the full universe of rules from which the best rule was drawn; the best rule generated superior performance in-sample even after the snooping adjustment, but did NOT provide superior performance in the subsequent 10-year post-sample period. Levich & Thomas (1993) applied a bootstrap to FX technical rule profits.

**Against:** Neely, Weller & Ulrich explicitly qualify the obituary: returns to LESS-STUDIED rules 'have declined but have probably not completely disappeared', and the adaptive-markets frame they endorse predicts new profit opportunities continue to be discovered and that complex strategies can stay profitable longer than simple ones. Osler (2003) independently supplies a microstructure MECHANISM for why support/resistance works, which is stronger evidence than a bare backtest. And Chang & Osler's 'Head and Shoulders: Not Just a Flaky Pattern' (FRBNY Staff Report No. 4, 1995) found the head-and-shoulders pattern had predictive ability for some dollar exchange rates over 1973-1994 - though I could NOT verify from any accessible source which pairs, what the profitability was net of costs, or whether it beat simpler rules, and I will not assert those details from memory. So the honest position is: 'assume snooped' is the right prior, not a proof.

**Survives retail costs?** Directly relevant to costs: STW's Reality Check and NWU's out-of-sample split are the two tests a retail backtest must pass before the cost arithmetic is even worth doing. A pattern's measured win rate on a single in-sample pass is not an input to p*; it is a number that has not yet been tested. Practical requirement for this trader's system: hold out data, test the FULL universe of rule variants tried (not the winner), and apply c=1.5 pips inside the backtest, not as an afterthought.

**Sources:** <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/adaptive-markets-hypothesis-evidence-from-the-foreign-exchange-market/9D336CDCA83233819EB5CDD0F4BC0DAA>, <https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fedlwp/2006-046.html>, <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/0022-1082.00163>, <https://www.kevinsheppard.com/files/teaching/mfe/advanced-econometrics/Sullivan_Timmermann_White.pdf>, <https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fednrp/9414.html>, <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=993938>, <https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2011/2011-001.pdf>

### Comparing spreads in pips across EURUSD, GBPUSD and USDJPY is misleading; converted to basis points of notional, a pip is worth materially different amounts.

**Quality:** model-knowledge-only (arithmetic, using approximate current price levels which I could not verify - all market data domains were blocked, so treat 1.10 / 1.27 / 150 as illustrative anchors and recompute at live prices).

**Evidence:** My arithmetic. 1 pip as a fraction of notional = pip_size / price: EURUSD at 1.10, 0.0001/1.10 = 0.909 bp; GBPUSD at 1.27, 0.0001/1.27 = 0.787 bp; USDJPY at 150, 0.01/150 = 0.667 bp. So a 1.2-pip EURUSD spread (1.09 bp) is a larger real cost than a 1.2-pip GBPUSD spread (0.94 bp) or a 1.2-pip USDJPY spread (0.80 bp). Also note USDJPY's dollar pip value is not $10 - at 150 it is 1,000 JPY = ~$6.67 per pip per standard lot - so pip-denominated P&L across pairs does not aggregate correctly either.

**Against:** Nothing contradicts the conversion, but the ranking flips with price level: if EURUSD trades at 1.30 a pip is 0.769 bp and it becomes cheaper than GBPUSD at 1.27. The point is not 'USDJPY is cheapest', it is 'the ranking depends on price and must be recomputed, not memorised'. A second caveat: GBPUSD's raw spread is typically 1.5-2x EURUSD's in pips, which more than offsets its lower bp-per-pip - so on realistic quoted spreads GBPUSD is still the more expensive of the two.

**Survives retail costs?** This is a modelling correction rather than an effect. Consequence for the system: express all costs and all edges in basis points inside the backtest, and convert to pips only at the order-entry layer.
